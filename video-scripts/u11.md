## 11.1 · The parabola
- **Video:** `u11-l01-the-parabola`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:21 (speech 2:25 · pauses 42 s · 363 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana recognizes the graph of a quadratic function as a parabola and identifies
its vertex, axis of symmetry, direction of opening, y-intercept and x-intercepts, using
symmetry to complete graphs.

**Topics.**
- `y = x²` from a table: the U shape.
- Opening up or down (sign of a).
- Vertex: minimum or maximum.
- Axis of symmetry; mirror points.
- y-intercept and x-intercepts (0, 1 or 2).

#### S1 · A new shape
*Screen:* table x: −3…3 · y = x²: 9, 4, 1, 0, 1, 4, 9; points plotted, joined in a U.
> Make a table for y equals x squared. Negative three gives nine, negative two gives four, negative one gives one, zero gives zero, and then one, four, nine again. Plot the points and join them smoothly.
> Notice anything about the y values?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the table, with equal y values linked by arcs; countdown ring.
> They repeat in mirror image: three and negative three both give nine. The curve is symmetric. This U shape is a parabola, the graph of every quadratic function.

#### S2 · The parts
*Screen:* `y = x² − 4x + 3` graphed; labels appear one by one.
> Here's the parabola of y equals x squared minus four x plus three. Let's name its parts.
*Screen:* vertex (2, −1) glows: "vertex (minimum)".
> The turning point is the vertex, two, negative one. This parabola opens up, so the vertex is its lowest point: a minimum.
*Screen:* dashed vertical line x = 2: "axis of symmetry".
> The vertical line through the vertex, x equals two, is the axis of symmetry. The two halves are mirror images across it.
*Screen:* (0, 3) "y-intercept"; (1, 0) and (3, 0) "x-intercepts (zeros)".
> It crosses the y axis at zero, three: the y intercept. It crosses the x axis at one and three: the x intercepts, or zeros.
> Look at the zeros, one and three, and the axis, x equals two. Is that a coincidence?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the zeros and the axis; countdown ring.
> No. By symmetry, the axis is exactly halfway between the two zeros. One and three average to two.

#### S3 · Up, down, and how many zeros
*Screen:* `y = −x² + 4`: opens down, vertex (0, 4) maximum.
> When the x squared term is negative, the parabola opens down, and the vertex is a maximum. Negative x squared plus four has its highest point at zero, four.
*Screen:* three parabolas: crossing the x-axis twice, touching once, missing it.
> A parabola can cross the x axis twice, touch it once at the vertex, or miss it completely. So a quadratic can have two zeros, one, or none.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* a parabola through (−1, 0), (5, 0), (0, −5), vertex (2, −9); and the point (4, ?) to find by symmetry.
> Your turn. For this parabola, name the vertex, the axis of symmetry, the zeros, the y intercept, and whether the vertex is a maximum or minimum. Then use symmetry to find the point with x equal to four. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* vertex (2, −9) min · axis x = 2 · zeros −1, 5 · y-int −5 · (4, −5) mirrors (0, −5).
> The vertex is two, negative nine, a minimum. The axis is x equals two, the zeros are negative one and five, and the y intercept is negative five. Four is the mirror of zero across x equals two, so the point is four, negative five.
> Remember: every parabola has a vertex, an axis of symmetry, and is a mirror image across it. Use the symmetry.

## 11.2 · Three forms of a quadratic
- **Video:** `u11-l02-three-forms`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:15 (speech 2:17 · pauses 42 s · 343 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana recognizes standard form `ax² + bx + c`, vertex form `a(x − h)² + k` and
factored form `a(x − p)(x − q)`, and converts vertex and factored forms to standard form by
expanding (and standard to factored by factoring).

**Topics.**
- One parabola, three equations.
- Standard form `y = ax² + bx + c`.
- Vertex form `y = a(x − h)² + k`.
- Factored form `y = a(x − p)(x − q)`.
- Converting by expanding and factoring.

#### S1 · Same curve, three names
*Screen:* one parabola; three equations appear next to it: `y = x² − 6x + 8` · `y = (x − 3)² − 1` · `y = (x − 2)(x − 4)`.
> These three equations look different. Yet they all describe the same parabola.
> How could you convince yourself they're really the same function?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
> Expand each one, or test a few x values. For x equals zero: eight, nine minus one is eight, and negative two times negative four is eight. Same output, and that works for every x.

#### S2 · The three forms
*Screen:* cards: Standard `ax² + bx + c` · Vertex `a(x − h)² + k` · Factored `a(x − p)(x − q)`.
> Standard form is a x squared plus b x plus c. Vertex form is a times the quantity x minus h, squared, plus k. Factored form is a times x minus p times x minus q.
> In all three, a is the same number: the coefficient of x squared. It decides whether the parabola opens up or down, and how wide it is.

#### S3 · To standard form
*Screen:* `y = 2(x + 1)² − 8` → `2(x² + 2x + 1) − 8` → `2x² + 4x + 2 − 8` → `2x² + 4x − 6`.
> To go from vertex form to standard form, expand. Two times the quantity x plus one squared, minus eight. Square first: x squared plus two x plus one. Then multiply by two and subtract eight: two x squared plus four x minus six.
> Why must you square the binomial before multiplying by two?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* order of operations badge "E before M"; countdown ring.
> Order of operations: the exponent belongs only to the parentheses, so it goes first.
*Screen:* `y = −(x − 1)(x − 5)` → `−(x² − 6x + 5)` → `−x² + 6x − 5`.
> Factored form also expands: negative one times x minus one times x minus five is negative x squared plus six x minus five.

#### S4 · From standard form
*Screen:* `y = 2x² + 4x − 6` → `2(x² + 2x − 3)` → `2(x + 3)(x − 1)`.
> Going back to factored form means factoring. Two x squared plus four x minus six: take out the two, then factor. Two times x plus three times x minus one.
> Getting from standard form to vertex form takes a method called completing the square, which you'll meet when solving quadratic equations.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) `y = −3(x − 2)² + 12` to standard form · (b) then to factored form.
> Your turn. Write negative three times the quantity x minus two squared, plus twelve, in standard form. Then factor it. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `−3(x² − 4x + 4) + 12 = −3x² + 12x` → `−3x(x − 4)`.
> Expand: negative three x squared plus twelve x minus twelve, plus twelve, which is negative three x squared plus twelve x. Factor: negative three x times x minus four.
> Remember: one parabola can be written in standard, vertex, or factored form, and you move between them by expanding or factoring.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 11.3 · What each form reveals
- **Video:** `u11-l03-what-each-form-reveals`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:12 (speech 2:16 · pauses 44 s · 339 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana reads the y-intercept from standard form, the vertex (max/min) from
vertex form, and the zeros from factored form; finds the vertex from standard form with
`x = −b/(2a)`; and chooses the form that answers a question.

**Topics.**
- Standard form → y-intercept c.
- Vertex form → vertex (h, k), max or min.
- Factored form → zeros p and q; axis halfway.
- `x = −b/(2a)` for the vertex from standard form.
- Matching questions to forms.

#### S1 · Each form has a superpower
*Screen:* the three equations of the same parabola from 11.2, each linked to one glowing feature.
> Each form of a quadratic makes one feature easy to see.
*Screen:* `y = x² − 6x + 8` → x = 0 → `y = 8`.
> In standard form, the constant c is the y intercept. Put x equal to zero: every x term vanishes, leaving eight.
*Screen:* `y = (x − 3)² − 1` → vertex (3, −1).
> In vertex form, a times the quantity x minus h squared plus k, the vertex is h, k. Here three, negative one.
> Why is the vertex at x equals three, and not negative three? And why is negative one the lowest value?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* `(x − 3)²` with a note "always ≥ 0"; countdown ring.
> The square, x minus three squared, is never negative. It's smallest, zero, when x is three. Then y equals zero minus one, negative one. Every other x adds something positive.
*Screen:* `y = (x − 2)(x − 4)` → zeros 2 and 4; axis x = 3.
> In factored form, the zeros jump out: x equals two and x equals four. And the axis of symmetry is halfway between them: three.

#### S2 · The vertex from standard form
*Screen:* `x = −b/(2a)`; `y = 2x² − 8x + 5` → `x = 8/4 = 2` → `y = 8 − 16 + 5 = −3` → vertex (2, −3).
> If you only have standard form, there's a formula for the vertex's x: negative b over two a. For two x squared minus eight x plus five, that's eight over four, two. Then plug in: y equals eight minus sixteen plus five, negative three. The vertex is two, negative three.

#### S3 · Pick the right tool
*Screen:* questions: "Where does the ball land?" · "What's the highest point?" · "What's the starting height?" with forms to match.
> Here are three questions about a thrown ball. Where does it land? What's its highest point? What height did it start from? Which form answers each one most directly?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the three questions and the three form cards; countdown ring.
> Landing means height zero: factored form. Highest point is the vertex: vertex form. Starting height is the value at time zero: standard form.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* `f(x) = −(x + 1)(x − 7)`: (a) zeros (b) axis (c) vertex (d) max or min (e) y-intercept.
> Your turn. For f of x equals negative one times x plus one times x minus seven, find the zeros, the axis of symmetry, the vertex, whether it's a maximum or minimum, and the y intercept. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* zeros −1, 7 · axis x = 3 · `f(3) = −(4)(−4) = 16` → vertex (3, 16), maximum · y-int `−(1)(−7) = 7`.
> The zeros are negative one and seven. The axis is halfway, x equals three. F of three is negative four times negative four with a minus, sixteen: the vertex is three, sixteen, a maximum since it opens down. The y intercept is seven.
> Remember: standard form shows the y intercept, vertex form shows the vertex, and factored form shows the zeros.

## 11.4 · Transformations of y = x²
- **Video:** `u11-l04-transformations`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:10 (speech 2:13 · pauses 42 s · 332 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana describes and graphs vertical and horizontal shifts, vertical stretches
and compressions, and reflections of `y = x²`, connecting them to `y = a(x − h)² + k`.

**Topics.**
- Vertical shift: `+ k`.
- Horizontal shift: `(x − h)`, and why the sign seems backwards.
- Stretch / compression: `|a| > 1`, `0 < |a| < 1`.
- Reflection: `a < 0`.
- Reading all of them from vertex form.

#### S1 · Moving the parent
*Screen:* `y = x²` (gray, the "parent"); then `y = x² + 3` slides up 3, `y = x² − 2` slides down 2.
> Start with the parent function, y equals x squared. Adding three to every output lifts the whole graph up three. Subtracting two moves it down two. Plus k shifts it vertically by k.

#### S2 · Sideways
*Screen:* `y = (x − 4)²`: the parabola moves right 4; vertex (4, 0).
> Now y equals the quantity x minus four, squared. The graph moves right four. Its vertex is four, zero.
> Minus four, but it moves to the right. Why does the sign seem backwards?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* a table: the original reaches 0 at x = 0; the new one reaches 0 at x = 4; countdown ring.
> The new function does what the old one did, four units later. The old one hit zero at x equals zero. The new one needs x equal to four to make x minus four equal zero. So everything happens four units to the right.
*Screen:* `y = (x + 1)²` moves left 1.
> And x plus one, squared, moves the graph left one.

#### S3 · Stretch, shrink, flip
*Screen:* `y = 3x²` narrow; `y = ½x²` wide; same vertex.
> Multiplying by a number stretches the graph vertically. Three x squared climbs three times as fast, so it looks narrower. One half x squared climbs half as fast, so it looks wider.
*Screen:* `y = −x²` reflected across the x-axis.
> A negative sign flips the graph across the x axis: negative x squared opens down.
*Screen:* `y = −2x²` vs. `y = 2x²`.
> Is negative two x squared narrower or wider than x squared?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
> Narrower. The sign flips it, but the size of a, two, decides the stretch.

#### S4 · All together
*Screen:* `y = −½(x + 3)² + 5`, built from the parent in steps: left 3, stretch by ½, flip, up 5 → vertex (−3, 5).
> Vertex form contains every move. Negative one half times the quantity x plus three squared, plus five: shift left three, compress by one half, flip so it opens down, and shift up five. The vertex is negative three, five.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* "Write the equation: the parent y = x² is shifted right 2, down 6, stretched by 4." and "Describe y = −(x − 1)² + 2."
> Your turn. Write the equation of y equals x squared shifted right two, down six, and stretched by four. Then describe the transformations in negative one times the quantity x minus one squared, plus two. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `y = 4(x − 2)² − 6` · "reflected, right 1, up 2; vertex (1, 2), opens down".
> Y equals four times the quantity x minus two squared, minus six. And the second is the parent flipped, moved right one and up two, with vertex one, two.
> Remember: k moves the graph up and down, h moves it left and right with the opposite sign, and a stretches, shrinks, or flips it.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 11.5 · Quadratic models
- **Video:** `u11-l05-quadratic-models`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:43 (speech 2:45 · pauses 44 s · 412 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana builds and interprets quadratic models for projectiles
(`h = −16t² + v₀t + h₀` or `−4.9t² …`) and for area problems, answering questions about
maximum values, intercepts and sensible domains.

**Topics.**
- Projectile model: meaning of each coefficient.
- Maximum height = vertex; landing time = zero.
- Area model: fixed perimeter fence, maximize area.
- Domain in context.

#### S1 · A thrown ball
*Screen:* a ball thrown upward from a 5 m balcony at 20 m/s; `h(t) = −4.9t² + 20t + 5`.
> A ball is thrown upward from a five meter balcony at twenty meters per second. Its height after t seconds is h of t equals negative four point nine t squared plus twenty t plus five.
> What does each number mean? Five is easy: the height at time zero. What about the other two?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the three coefficients glow; countdown ring.
> Twenty is the starting upward speed. Negative four point nine comes from gravity pulling the ball down. It's why the graph opens down.

#### S2 · Highest point and landing
*Screen:* `t = −b/(2a) = −20/(−9.8) ≈ 2.04 s` → `h(2.04) ≈ 25.4 m`.
> The highest point is the vertex. Negative b over two a is negative twenty over negative nine point eight, about two point zero four seconds. The height then is about twenty-five point four meters.
*Screen:* the graph crossing zero at t ≈ 4.32; the negative root crossed out.
> It lands when h is zero, at about four point three two seconds. The equation has another solution, a negative time, but that's before the ball was thrown, so we ignore it. The domain in this context is from zero to about four point three two seconds.

#### S3 · The biggest pen
*Screen:* 40 m of fence for a rectangular pen against a wall (3 sides fenced); width x, length 40 − 2x.
> A farmer has forty meters of fence to make a rectangular pen against a barn wall, so only three sides need fence. If each short side is x, the long side is forty minus two x.
*Screen:* `A(x) = x(40 − 2x) = −2x² + 40x`; graph, vertex (10, 200).
> The area is x times the quantity forty minus two x: negative two x squared plus forty x. It's a parabola opening down, so it has a maximum. The vertex is at x equals negative forty over negative four, ten. A of ten is two hundred.
*Screen:* pen drawn 10 × 20.
> The biggest pen is ten by twenty meters, with an area of two hundred square meters.
> What values of x make sense here? What happens at x equals zero, and at x equals twenty?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the pen squeezing to nothing at both ends; countdown ring.
> At zero or twenty the pen has no area. Only x between zero and twenty makes a real pen.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* "A rocket is launched from the ground: h(t) = −16t² + 128t (feet, seconds). Find its maximum height and when it lands."
> Your turn. A toy rocket is launched from the ground, with height h of t equals negative sixteen t squared plus one hundred twenty-eight t, in feet. Find its maximum height and when it lands. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `t = −128/(−32) = 4` → `h(4) = −256 + 512 = 256 ft` · `−16t(t − 8) = 0` → lands at t = 8 s.
> The vertex is at t equals four seconds, and h of four is two hundred fifty-six feet. Factor: negative sixteen t times t minus eight. It lands at eight seconds, twice the time to the top, by symmetry.
> Remember: in quadratic models, the vertex answers "biggest" or "highest", the zeros answer "when does it hit zero", and the context decides the domain.
> That's the end of quadratic functions. Next, solving quadratic equations.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.
