## 13.1 · Choosing a model
- **Video:** `u13-l01-choosing-a-model`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:06 (speech 2:06 · pauses 47 s · 314 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana decides whether data or a situation is best modeled by a linear,
exponential or quadratic function, using differences, ratios, second differences, scatter
plots, residuals and the context.

**Topics.**
- First differences constant → linear.
- Ratios constant → exponential.
- Second differences constant → quadratic.
- Shape of the scatter plot and residual plots.
- Context: does it make sense?

#### S1 · Three tables
*Screen:* three tables, x = 0…4: A: 3, 7, 11, 15, 19 · B: 3, 6, 12, 24, 48 · C: 3, 4, 7, 12, 19.
> Here are three tables. They all start at three. One is linear, one is exponential, and one is quadratic.
> Before I tell you, can you sort them? What would you check?
[REFLECT 15s] *Screen:* the tables; countdown ring.

#### S2 · Differences and ratios
*Screen:* A: differences 4, 4, 4, 4 → linear.
> In table A, the differences are all four. Equal first differences: linear.
*Screen:* B: differences 3, 6, 12, 24 ✗ · ratios 2, 2, 2, 2 → exponential.
> In table B the differences grow, but the ratios are all two. Equal ratios: exponential.
*Screen:* C: first differences 1, 3, 5, 7 → second differences 2, 2, 2 → quadratic.
> In table C, neither works. But look at the differences of the differences: three minus one, five minus three, seven minus five. All two. Equal second differences mean quadratic.
> Why would a quadratic have constant second differences? Think about x squared: zero, one, four, nine, sixteen.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* x²: 0, 1, 4, 9, 16 → 1, 3, 5, 7 → 2, 2, 2; countdown ring.
> The first differences of x squared are the odd numbers, one, three, five, seven, which form a linear pattern. A linear pattern has constant differences. So a quadratic has constant second differences.

#### S3 · Real data is messy
*Screen:* a scatter plot of real data (a cooling cup of tea: temperature over 30 minutes), with a line and an exponential fitted; the line's residual plot shows a U shape, the exponential's is random.
> Real data never fits perfectly. Here's a cup of tea cooling over thirty minutes. A line and an exponential decay are both fitted. The residuals of the line form a clear U shape; the residuals of the exponential look random.
> The exponential is the better model. And it makes sense: tea cools fast at first and then slower, approaching room temperature.
*Screen:* checklist: shape of the scatter plot · differences or ratios · residual plot · context.
> So use everything: the shape, the differences or ratios, the residuals, and whether the model makes sense in the story.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) a ball's height every 0.5 s: 2, 9.8, 15.1, 17.9, 18.2, 16.0 · (b) a savings account growing 3% a year · (c) a taxi: $3 plus $2 per mile.
> Your turn. Choose linear, exponential, or quadratic for each one, and say why. A ball's height every half second. A savings account that grows three percent a year. A taxi that charges three dollars plus two dollars per mile. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* (a) quadratic: rises then falls, roughly constant second differences · (b) exponential: constant percent · (c) linear: constant rate.
> The ball rises and falls, with roughly constant second differences: quadratic. The savings grow by a constant percent: exponential. The taxi adds a constant amount per mile: linear.
> Remember: equal differences, linear; equal ratios, exponential; equal second differences, quadratic. Then check the residuals and the context.

## 13.2 · Combining functions
- **Video:** `u13-l02-combining-functions`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:40 (speech 2:43 · pauses 44 s · 407 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana builds new functions by adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing
functions (profit = revenue − cost, total cost = price × quantity), evaluates them, and
interprets the result in context.

**Topics.**
- `(f + g)(x)`, `(f − g)(x)`, `(f · g)(x)`, `(f/g)(x)`.
- Profit = revenue − cost.
- Revenue = price × quantity (a quadratic appears).
- Average cost = total cost ÷ quantity; domain restrictions.

#### S1 · A lemonade stand
*Screen:* `R(x) = 2x` (revenue, $2 per cup) · `C(x) = 0.5x + 15` (cost: $15 setup + $0.50 per cup).
> A lemonade stand sells cups for two dollars. So the revenue from x cups is R of x equals two x. The costs are fifteen dollars to set up, plus fifty cents per cup: C of x equals zero point five x plus fifteen.
> How would you write the profit as a function of x?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* both functions; countdown ring.
*Screen:* `P(x) = R(x) − C(x) = 2x − (0.5x + 15) = 1.5x − 15`.
> Profit is revenue minus cost: two x minus the quantity zero point five x plus fifteen. That's one point five x minus fifteen. We just subtracted two functions to build a new one.
*Screen:* `P(10) = 0` → break-even at 10 cups; `P(40) = 45`.
> P of ten is zero: ten cups is the break-even point. P of forty is forty-five dollars of profit.

#### S2 · The four operations
*Screen:* `(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)`, and likewise −, ·, ÷; `f(x) = x + 3`, `g(x) = x²` worked: sum `x² + x + 3`, product `x³ + 3x²`.
> In general, f plus g of x means f of x plus g of x, and the same for subtracting, multiplying, and dividing. With f of x equals x plus three and g of x equals x squared, the sum is x squared plus x plus three, and the product is x cubed plus three x squared.

#### S3 · Multiplying creates a quadratic
*Screen:* a theater: price `p(x) = 20 − x` dollars after x one-dollar discounts; tickets sold `q(x) = 200 + 20x`; revenue `R(x) = (20 − x)(200 + 20x)`.
> A theater sells two hundred tickets at twenty dollars. For each dollar it drops the price, it sells twenty more. After x discounts, the price is twenty minus x and the number sold is two hundred plus twenty x.
*Screen:* `R(x) = −20x² + 200x + 4000`; vertex x = 5 → price $15, 300 tickets, $4,500.
> Revenue is price times quantity, the product of two linear functions: negative twenty x squared plus two hundred x plus four thousand. A quadratic! Its vertex, at x equals five, gives the best price, fifteen dollars, with four thousand five hundred dollars of revenue.
> Why does lowering the price first raise the revenue, and then lower it?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the parabola of revenue; countdown ring.
> At first, the extra tickets sold more than make up for the lower price. But past fifteen dollars, each discount loses more than the new tickets bring in.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* `C(x) = 4x + 600` (cost to make x shirts) · (a) average cost `A(x) = C(x)/x` (b) `A(100)` (c) what x is not allowed?
> Your turn. It costs C of x equals four x plus six hundred dollars to make x shirts. Write the average cost per shirt as a function, find it for one hundred shirts, and say which value of x is not allowed. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `A(x) = (4x + 600)/x` · `A(100) = 1000/100 = $10` · x = 0 not allowed (division by zero; and you can't average over zero shirts).
> A of x equals four x plus six hundred, over x. For one hundred shirts, one thousand over one hundred: ten dollars each. X can't be zero: you can't divide by zero, and there's no average cost of no shirts.
> Remember: functions can be added, subtracted, multiplied, and divided, and in real problems, the new function usually has its own meaning, like profit or revenue.

## 13.3 · Capstone project with real data
- **Video:** `u13-l03-capstone`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:14 (speech 2:01 · pauses 60 s · 302 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana knows what the capstone project asks for and how to carry it out:
choose a question, collect real data, choose and fit a model, use it to predict, and judge
its limits, presenting the reasoning clearly.

**Topics.**
- The question and the data (at least 10 data points, with a source).
- Plotting and choosing a model type.
- Fitting, residuals, and r where it applies.
- Predicting, and the limits of the model.
- What the final presentation includes.

#### S1 · Your own question
*Screen:* example questions: "How fast is my phone's battery draining?" · "How did Kentucky's population grow?" · "How far does a paper airplane fly when I change its wing size?" · "How does the price of a used car fall with age?"
> This whole course has been building to this: using algebra on a question you care about. In the capstone project, you choose a question, gather real data, and build a model.
> Take a moment. What's something in your life, or in the news, that changes in a way you could measure?
[REFLECT 15s] *Screen:* the example questions; countdown ring.

#### S2 · The steps
*Screen:* five steps appear as a path: 1 Question · 2 Data · 3 Model · 4 Predict · 5 Judge.
> The project has five steps.
> First, the question. It must be about two quantities that change together, like time and battery percent.
> Second, the data. Collect at least ten data points, by measuring or from a trustworthy source, and say where they came from.
*Screen:* a scatter plot of example battery data.
> Third, the model. Plot the data. Use what you know, differences, ratios, the shape, and the context, to choose linear, exponential, or quadratic. Fit it with technology, and check the residuals.
> Fourth, predict. Use your model to answer your question, like when the battery will reach zero.
> Fifth, judge. Where does your model work, and where does it stop making sense?
*Screen:* the battery model extended past 0% into negative values, in red.
> For example, a battery model can't predict a negative percent. Saying where a model breaks is part of a good answer, not a weakness.

#### S3 · What makes it good
*Screen:* a rubric: clear question · real data with source · justified model choice · correct fit and residual check · prediction with units · honest limits · clear presentation.
> A strong project explains why the model was chosen, not just which one. It shows the residuals. It gives predictions with units. And it is honest about what the model can't do.
> Here's a question to keep in mind while you work: if someone collected new data tomorrow, would your model still fit? What would you expect to change?
[REFLECT 15s] *Screen:* the rubric; countdown ring.

#### S4 · Get started
*Screen:* a planning card to fill in: "My question" · "My two quantities" · "Where my data will come from" · "My first guess at the model".
> Your first task is the planning card: your question, your two quantities, where your data will come from, and your first guess at the model. Pause the video and fill it in now.
[TRY IT 30s] *Screen:* the planning card; countdown ring.
> When the card is done, start collecting. Good luck. This is real mathematics: asking a question about the world and answering it with evidence.
