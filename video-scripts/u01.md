## 1.1 · One-step and multi-step equations
- **Video:** `u01-l01-solving-equations`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 6:14 (speech 4:13 · pauses 90 s · 633 words · 9 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana understands an equation as a balance, solves one-step equations with inverse operations, and extends the same idea to multi-step equations (distributing, combining like terms, clearing fractions), checking every answer by substitution.

**Topics.**
- What a solution is; the balance: do the same to both sides.
- Inverse operations: add ↔ subtract, multiply ↔ divide.
- Checking by substitution.
- Undoing in reverse order (shoes and socks); two-step equations.
- Simplify each side first: distribute and combine.
- Clearing fractions with a common denominator.

#### S1 · A mystery number
*Screen:* `x + 7 = 12`, with a box over x.
> I'm thinking of a number. When I add seven, I get twelve. What's my number?
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `5 + 7 = 12` ✓.
> Five. You probably undid the plus seven in your head. Solving an equation means finding the value that makes it true, and that value is called the solution.

#### S2 · The balance
*Screen:* a balance scale: left pan has an x-bag and 7 unit blocks, right pan 12 blocks; it is level.
> Think of an equation as a balance. Both sides weigh the same.
*Screen:* 7 blocks removed from each pan; the scale stays level: `x = 5`.
> To get x alone, take seven away from the left. To keep it balanced, take seven away from the right too. What's left is x equals five.
> Why do we have to do the same thing to both sides? What would happen to the balance if we didn't?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the scale tilting when blocks are removed from one side only.
> It would tip. The two sides would no longer be equal, and the equation would become false. Doing the same to both sides is the one rule that keeps it true.

#### S3 · Inverse operations
*Screen:* table: `+ 7` undone by `− 7` · `− 4` by `+ 4` · `× 3` by `÷ 3` · `÷ 2` by `× 2`.
> Every operation has an inverse that undoes it. Addition and subtraction undo each other. Multiplication and division undo each other.
*Screen:* `3x = 21` → `3x ÷ 3 = 21 ÷ 3` → `x = 7`; the `÷ 3` written on both sides in yellow.
> In three x equals twenty-one, x is multiplied by three. Undo it by dividing both sides by three. X equals seven.
*Screen:* `x − 4 = −9` → `x = −5`.
> In x minus four equals negative nine, undo the minus four by adding four to both sides. X equals negative five.
*Screen:* `x/2 = 6` → `x = 12`.
> And in x over two equals six, multiply both sides by two. X equals twelve.

#### S4 · Always check
*Screen:* `x − 4 = −9`, x = −5 substituted: `−5 − 4 = −9` ✓.
> You can always check a solution. Put it back into the original equation. Negative five minus four is negative nine. It works.
> Here's one to think about. In negative x equals eight, what is x multiplied by?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `−x = 8`; countdown ring.
*Screen:* `−1 · x = 8` → divide by −1 → `x = −8`.
> By negative one. Divide both sides by negative one, and x equals negative eight.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* three equations: `y + 15 = 4` · `−6m = 42` · `k/5 = −3`.
> Your turn. Solve all three, and check each one. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* answers with checks: `y = −11` · `m = −7` · `k = −15`.
> Y equals negative eleven, m equals negative seven, and k equals negative fifteen.
> Remember: find the operation done to the variable, undo it with its inverse, do it to both sides, and check.

#### S6 · Shoes and socks
*Screen:* animation: socks on, then shoes; to undo, shoes off first, then socks.
> In the morning you put on socks, then shoes. At night you undo it in reverse: shoes first, then socks.
*Screen:* `2x + 3 = 11` with the history "x → times 2 → plus 3".
> Equations work the same way. In two x plus three equals eleven, x was multiplied by two and then three was added. So we undo in reverse.
> Which operation do we undo first?
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `2x = 8` → `x = 4`.
> The last one, plus three. Subtract three from both sides: two x equals eight. Then divide by two: x equals four.

#### S7 · Clean up first
*Screen:* `3(x − 2) + x = 18`.
> Some equations need tidying before you undo anything. Three times the quantity x minus two, plus x, equals eighteen.
*Screen:* `3x − 6 + x = 18` (DIST yellow) → `4x − 6 = 18` (like terms).
> Distribute: three x minus six plus x. Combine like terms: four x minus six equals eighteen.
*Screen:* `4x = 24` → `x = 6`; check `3(4) + 6 = 18` ✓.
> Now it's a two-step equation. Add six: four x equals twenty-four. Divide by four: x equals six. And the check works: three times four, plus six, is eighteen.
> Why is it safer to simplify each side before moving anything across the equals sign?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
> Because then each side is as short as it can be, and every step you take is a single, clear move.

#### S8 · Fractions
*Screen:* `x/3 + 1/2 = 5/6`.
> Fractions make equations look harder than they are. Look at x over three, plus one half, equals five sixths.
> What number could you multiply everything by, so that every fraction disappears?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* denominators 3, 2, 6 glow; countdown ring.
*Screen:* `6 · x/3 + 6 · 1/2 = 6 · 5/6` → `2x + 3 = 5` → `x = 1`.
> Six, because six is a multiple of three, two, and six. Multiply every term on both sides by six. You get two x plus three equals five. Subtract three, divide by two, and x equals one.

#### S9 · Your turn
*Screen:* `5 − 2(x + 4) = 7`.
> Your turn. Solve five minus two times the quantity x plus four equals seven. Watch the sign of the two. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `5 − 2x − 8 = 7` → `−2x − 3 = 7` → `−2x = 10` → `x = −5`.
> Distribute negative two: five minus two x minus eight. Combine: negative two x minus three equals seven. Add three: negative two x equals ten. Divide by negative two: x equals negative five.
> Remember: simplify each side, then undo the operations in reverse order, and check your answer in the original equation.

## 1.2 · Variables on both sides; no solution and infinitely many
- **Video:** `u01-l02-variables-on-both-sides`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 5:51 (speech 3:56 · pauses 87 s · 589 words · 8 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana solves equations with the variable on both sides, interprets the solution in context, and recognizes when an equation has no solution (a false statement remains) or infinitely many (an identity).

**Topics.**
- Comparing two plans: why the variable appears on both sides.
- Collect variable terms on one side, constants on the other; choosing the easier side.
- When the variable disappears: false → no solution, true → identity.
- Predicting the number of solutions from coefficients and constants.

#### S1 · Two gyms
*Screen:* two gym cards: Gym A $20 fee + $5 per visit · Gym B $8 per visit.
> Gym A charges twenty dollars to join, plus five dollars a visit. Gym B has no fee, but charges eight dollars a visit. After how many visits do they cost the same?
*Screen:* `20 + 5v = 8v`.
> Let v be the number of visits. The costs are equal when twenty plus five v equals eight v. Now v is on both sides.
> Which gym would you guess is cheaper for someone who goes only twice? And for someone who goes twenty times?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* both cards; countdown ring.

#### S2 · Collect the variable
*Screen:* `20 + 5v = 8v` → subtract 5v from both sides → `20 = 3v`.
> To solve it, gather all the v terms on one side. Subtract five v from both sides. Twenty equals three v.
*Screen:* `v = 20/3 ≈ 6.67`.
> Divide by three. V equals twenty over three, about six point six seven.
*Screen:* a cost table for 6 and 7 visits: A $50 / $55, B $48 / $56.
> Visits come in whole numbers, so look at six and seven. At six visits, Gym B is cheaper. At seven, Gym A is cheaper. The equation tells us exactly where they switch.

#### S3 · Choosing a side
*Screen:* `7x − 4 = 3x + 12`.
> Here's another. Seven x minus four equals three x plus twelve. You could move either x term. Which choice makes the arithmetic easier?
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* subtract 3x: `4x − 4 = 12` → `4x = 16` → `x = 4`.
> Subtracting three x keeps a positive coefficient: four x minus four equals twelve. Add four, divide by four, and x equals four.
*Screen:* the other route: `−4 = −4x + 12` → `−16 = −4x` → `x = 4`.
> Moving seven x instead also works, and gives the same answer. It's just more negative signs to handle.
> Why must both routes give the same answer?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* both routes side by side, both ending in x = 4.
> Because every step kept the equation true. The solution belongs to the equation, not to the route you took.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* `2(3x − 1) = 4x + 10`.
> Your turn. Solve two times the quantity three x minus one, equals four x plus ten. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `6x − 2 = 4x + 10` → `2x − 2 = 10` → `2x = 12` → `x = 6`.
> Distribute: six x minus two equals four x plus ten. Subtract four x: two x minus two equals ten. Add two, divide by two. X equals six.
> Remember: simplify each side, collect the variable terms on one side and the numbers on the other, then finish as usual.

#### S5 · The variable vanishes
*Screen:* `3x + 5 = 3x + 2`.
> Try to solve three x plus five equals three x plus two.
*Screen:* subtract 3x: `5 = 2`, in red.
> Subtract three x from both sides, and you get five equals two. The x is gone, and what's left is false.
> What does that tell you about x?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `5 = 2` with a question mark; countdown ring.
> No value of x can make this equation true. Whatever x is, the left side is always three more than the right side. The equation has no solution.

#### S6 · Always true
*Screen:* `2(x + 3) = 2x + 6`.
> Now try two times the quantity x plus three, equals two x plus six.
*Screen:* `2x + 6 = 2x + 6` → `6 = 6`, in green.
> Distribute: two x plus six equals two x plus six. Subtract two x: six equals six. That's true, always.
*Screen:* a table: x = −3, 0, 4, 10 → both sides equal.
> Every number works. Try any value of x and both sides match. This equation has infinitely many solutions. An equation like this is called an identity.

#### S7 · Seeing it coming
*Screen:* three equations in a column, each simplified to `ax + b = cx + d` form.
> You can predict the outcome before solving. Simplify each side to some number times x, plus a constant.
*Screen:* "different coefficients → one solution" · "same coefficients, different constants → no solution" · "same coefficients, same constants → infinitely many".
> If the x coefficients are different, there's exactly one solution. If they're the same but the constants differ, there's none. If both match, there are infinitely many.
*Screen:* `4x − 1 = 4x + k`.
> Here's a thinking question. In four x minus one equals four x plus k, what value of k gives infinitely many solutions? And which values give none?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
> K equal to negative one makes both sides identical. Any other k makes it impossible.

#### S8 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) `5x − 2(x + 1) = 3x − 2` · (b) `6x + 4 = 2(3x + 1)`.
> Your turn. Decide how many solutions each equation has. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* (a) `3x − 2 = 3x − 2` → infinitely many · (b) `6x + 4 = 6x + 2` → no solution.
> The first simplifies to three x minus two on both sides, so infinitely many solutions. The second becomes six x plus four equals six x plus two, which is impossible. No solution.
> Remember: when the variable disappears, read what's left. False means no solution. True means every number is a solution.

## 1.3 · Justifying each step
- **Video:** `u01-l03-properties-of-equality`
- **Status:** produced
- **Estimated length:** 2:40 (speech 1:49 · pauses 40 s · 272 words · 4 scenes) · short

**Objective.** Ana names the property that justifies each step of a solution (addition,
subtraction, multiplication, division properties of equality, distributive property,
combining like terms) and spots the step where a wrong solution breaks.

**Topics.**
- Why justify: a solution is an argument.
- The properties of equality.
- A two-column proof of a solution.
- Finding the error in someone else's work.

#### S1 · Proving it
*Screen:* a short solution without reasons: `4x + 3 = 19` → `4x = 16` → `x = 4`.
> Here's a solution. It's correct. But imagine someone asks you, why is each step allowed?
> Take a moment. What would you say about the first step?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the arrow from line 1 to line 2 glows; countdown ring.
> In math, each step needs a reason. Solving an equation is really a short argument, and the reasons are called properties.

#### S2 · The properties of equality
*Screen:* four cards: Addition, Subtraction, Multiplication, Division property of equality, each with `if a = b, then a + c = b + c` and so on.
> The properties of equality say the balance stays level. If two things are equal, you can add the same number to both, subtract the same number, multiply both by the same number, or divide both by the same number, as long as it isn't zero.
> Why do you think dividing by zero has to be excluded?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `÷ 0` crossed out; countdown ring.
> Because division by zero has no answer at all. A step that produces no number can't keep anything equal.

#### S3 · A two-column solution
*Screen:* two-column table, Statement · Reason:
`3(x − 2) = 12` · Given;
`3x − 6 = 12` · Distributive property;
`3x = 18` · Addition property of equality;
`x = 6` · Division property of equality.
> Here is a full solution with its reasons. Three times the quantity x minus two equals twelve is given. Distributing gives three x minus six. Adding six to both sides is the addition property of equality. Dividing both sides by three is the division property. Each line follows from the line before it.

#### S4 · Find the mistake
*Screen:* a student's work: `2x + 5 = 3x − 1` → `5 = x − 1` → `4 = x`; the last step in red.
> Here's a student's work on two x plus five equals three x minus one. The answer is wrong. Find the step that breaks, and name the property that should have been used.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* corrected: `5 = x − 1` → add 1 → `6 = x`.
> The first step is fine: subtracting two x from both sides. The error is the last step. From five equals x minus one, you must add one to both sides, by the addition property of equality. X equals six, not four.
> Remember: every step is a move with a name. If you can't name it, it probably isn't allowed.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 1.4 · Linear inequalities and their graphs
- **Video:** `u01-l04-inequalities`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 5:34 (speech 3:49 · pauses 79 s · 572 words · 8 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana reads, writes and graphs inequalities as sets of numbers (open and closed circles), solves linear inequalities like equations, and reverses the symbol when multiplying or dividing by a negative number, explaining why.

**Topics.**
- The four inequality symbols; solution sets.
- Graphing: open circle (strict) vs. closed circle (or equal).
- Solving with the same moves as equations.
- Why multiplying by a negative flips the order; test points.

#### S1 · More than one answer
*Screen:* a ride sign "You must be at least 48 inches tall".
> A ride says you must be at least forty-eight inches tall. Who can ride?
> Is there one answer, or many?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `h ≥ 48`.
> Many. Anyone forty-eight inches or taller. We write h is greater than or equal to forty-eight. An inequality has a whole set of solutions.

#### S2 · The symbols
*Screen:* `<` less than · `>` greater than · `≤` less than or equal · `≥` greater than or equal.
> There are four symbols: less than, greater than, less than or equal to, and greater than or equal to. The small end points to the smaller number.
*Screen:* "at least" → `≥` · "at most" → `≤` · "more than" → `>` · "fewer than" → `<`.
> In words, at least means greater than or equal to. At most means less than or equal to.

#### S3 · Drawing the set
*Screen:* number line; `x > 2`: open circle at 2, arrow to the right.
> To graph x is greater than two, put a circle at two and shade everything to the right. The circle is open, because two itself is not greater than two.
*Screen:* `x ≤ −1`: closed circle at −1, arrow to the left.
> For x is less than or equal to negative one, the circle is filled in, because negative one is included. Then shade to the left.
> Is two point zero zero one a solution of x greater than two? What's the smallest solution?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* zoom into the number line near 2; countdown ring.
> Two point zero zero one is a solution. But there's no smallest one: whatever number you pick above two, there's another one closer. That's why the open circle shows a boundary that isn't reached.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) "a team can have at most 12 players" · (b) a graph: closed circle at −3, shaded right.
> Your turn. Write an inequality for the first sentence, and the inequality shown by the graph. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 15s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* (a) `p ≤ 12` · (b) `x ≥ −3`.
> At most twelve players is p less than or equal to twelve. The graph shows x greater than or equal to negative three.
> Remember: an inequality describes a set of numbers, open circles leave the boundary out, closed circles keep it in.

#### S5 · Like an equation
*Screen:* `x + 4 < 10` → `x < 6`, graph with open circle at 6.
> Solving an inequality is almost like solving an equation. In x plus four is less than ten, subtract four from both sides. X is less than six.
*Screen:* `3x ≥ 12` → `x ≥ 4`.
> And in three x is greater than or equal to twelve, divide by three. X is greater than or equal to four.

#### S6 · The flip
*Screen:* `2 < 5` true; multiply both by −1: `−2 ? −5`; number line shows −2 to the right of −5.
> But there's one exception. Two is less than five. Multiply both sides by negative one. Is negative two less than negative five?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the number line with both pairs marked; countdown ring.
*Screen:* `−2 > −5`; arrows show the mirror image around 0.
> No. Negative two is greater than negative five. Multiplying by a negative number reflects the numbers across zero, so their order reverses.
*Screen:* rule card in red: "× or ÷ by a negative → flip the symbol".
> So whenever you multiply or divide both sides by a negative number, you must flip the inequality symbol.

#### S7 · A multi-step example
*Screen:* `5 − 3x > 17`.
> Let's solve five minus three x is greater than seventeen.
*Screen:* `−3x > 12` → `x < −4` (the symbol flips, red flash) → graph: open circle at −4, shaded left.
> Subtract five: negative three x is greater than twelve. Divide by negative three, and flip the symbol: x is less than negative four.
*Screen:* test point x = −5: `5 + 15 = 20 > 17` ✓; x = 0: `5 > 17` ✗.
> Check with a test point. Negative five is in the shaded part: five plus fifteen is twenty, which is greater than seventeen. Zero is not shaded, and five isn't greater than seventeen. The graph is right.
> Could you avoid the flip by moving the x term to the other side instead? Try it in your head.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* `5 − 3x > 17` → `5 − 17 > 3x`; countdown ring.
> Yes. Adding three x and subtracting seventeen gives negative twelve is greater than three x, so x is less than negative four. Same answer, no flip needed.

#### S8 · Your turn
*Screen:* `−2(x − 1) ≤ 8`.
> Your turn. Solve and graph negative two times the quantity x minus one, is less than or equal to eight. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `−2x + 2 ≤ 8` → `−2x ≤ 6` → `x ≥ −3`; closed circle at −3, shaded right.
> Distribute: negative two x plus two. Subtract two: negative two x is less than or equal to six. Divide by negative two and flip: x is greater than or equal to negative three.
> Remember: solve as usual, flip the symbol when you multiply or divide by a negative, and check with a test point.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 1.5 · Compound inequalities
- **Video:** `u01-l05-compound-inequalities`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:00 (speech 2:06 · pauses 42 s · 314 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana solves and graphs compound inequalities joined by *and* (intersection,
often written `a < x < b`) and *or* (union), and interprets them in context.

**Topics.**
- "And": both conditions at once; the overlap.
- Three-part form `a < x < b`, solved all at once.
- "Or": either condition; the union.
- Cases with no solution or all numbers.

#### S1 · A safe range
*Screen:* a thermometer: a medicine must be kept above 2 °C and below 8 °C.
> A medicine must be kept warmer than two degrees and colder than eight degrees. Which temperatures are safe?
*Screen:* `t > 2 and t < 8` → `2 < t < 8`; the two graphs overlap, the overlap stays shaded.
> Both conditions must hold at the same time. We write it as two is less than t, which is less than eight. On the number line, it's the overlap of the two graphs.

#### S2 · Solving three parts at once
*Screen:* `−1 ≤ 2x + 3 < 9`.
> In negative one is less than or equal to two x plus three, which is less than nine, the variable sits in the middle. Whatever you do, do it to all three parts.
*Screen:* `−4 ≤ 2x < 6` → `−2 ≤ x < 3`; closed circle at −2, open at 3, shaded between.
> Subtract three from all three parts: negative four, two x, six. Divide all three by two: negative two is less than or equal to x, which is less than three.
> Why does one end have a closed circle and the other an open one?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* both circles glow; countdown ring.
> Because the left symbol includes equality and the right one doesn't. Each end keeps its own symbol.

#### S3 · Or
*Screen:* "A store gives a discount to children under 12 or adults 65 and over." `a < 12 or a ≥ 65`; two rays in opposite directions.
> Now the word or. A discount for children under twelve or adults sixty-five and over. A person qualifies if either condition is true. The graph is both pieces together, pointing away from each other.
*Screen:* `x < 3 or x > 1` → whole line shaded; `x > 5 and x < 2` → empty.
> Here are two puzzles. What numbers satisfy x less than three or x greater than one? And what numbers satisfy x greater than five and x less than two?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* both puzzles; countdown ring.
> Every number satisfies the first: each number is either less than three or greater than one. No number satisfies the second: nothing is both greater than five and less than two.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* `3x − 1 < −7 or 2x + 5 ≥ 11`.
> Your turn. Solve and graph three x minus one is less than negative seven, or two x plus five is greater than or equal to eleven. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `x < −2 or x ≥ 3`; open circle at −2 shaded left; closed at 3 shaded right.
> The first part gives x less than negative two. The second gives x greater than or equal to three. The solution is both rays.
> Remember: and means both, the overlap. Or means either, everything in both graphs.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 1.6 · Literal equations
- **Video:** `u01-l06-literal-equations`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 2:52 (speech 2:02 · pauses 37 s · 306 words · 4 scenes) · short

**Objective.** Ana solves a formula for one of its variables using the same moves as for
equations, and explains why the rearranged formula is useful.

**Topics.**
- Formulas are equations with several letters.
- Solving `A = ½bh` for `h`.
- Solving `y = mx + b` for `x`; `P = 2l + 2w` for `w`.
- Checking with numbers.

#### S1 · Many triangles
*Screen:* `A = ½bh`, a triangle with base and height labeled.
> The area of a triangle is one half times base times height. Suppose you know the area and the base of many triangles, and you need the height of each one.
> Would you rather solve a new equation every time, or have one formula that gives the height directly?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* a list of five triangles with A and b given; countdown ring.
> One formula. Solving a formula for a variable is called solving a literal equation, and it uses exactly the moves you already know.

#### S2 · Solve for h
*Screen:* `A = ½bh` → multiply both sides by 2 → `2A = bh` → divide by b → `h = 2A/b`.
> We want h alone. H is multiplied by one half and by b. Multiply both sides by two: two A equals b h. Divide both sides by b: h equals two A over b.
*Screen:* check: A = 12, b = 6 → `h = 24/6 = 4`; ½ · 6 · 4 = 12 ✓.
> Check it with numbers. If the area is twelve and the base is six, the height is twenty-four over six, which is four. And one half of six times four is twelve. It works.

#### S3 · Treat letters like numbers
*Screen:* `P = 2l + 2w`, solve for w; next to it, the same moves on `30 = 2(9) + 2w`.
> Now solve the perimeter formula, P equals two l plus two w, for w. If you're unsure, solve a version with numbers next to it, and do the same moves.
*Screen:* `P − 2l = 2w` → `w = (P − 2l)/2`.
> Subtract two l from both sides: P minus two l equals two w. Divide by two: w equals the quantity P minus two l, over two.
> Is it also correct to write w equals P over two, minus l? Think about why.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* both forms side by side; countdown ring.
> Yes. Dividing a difference by two divides each part by two. Both forms are the same formula.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* `y = mx + b`, solve for x.
> Your turn. Solve y equals m x plus b for x. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 15s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `y − b = mx` → `x = (y − b)/m`, with a note "m ≠ 0".
> Subtract b: y minus b equals m x. Divide by m: x equals the quantity y minus b, over m. That works as long as m is not zero.
> Remember: in a literal equation, the other letters are just numbers you don't know yet. Use the same inverse operations, and check with real values.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 1.7 · Units and dimensional analysis
- **Video:** `u01-l07-units`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:08 (speech 2:11 · pauses 42 s · 327 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana converts units by multiplying by conversion factors equal to one, keeps
track of units through calculations, and uses units to check that an answer makes sense.

**Topics.**
- Units are part of the quantity.
- Conversion factors equal to one.
- Chaining factors (miles per hour → feet per second).
- Checking formulas with units.

#### S2 · Multiplying by one
*Screen:* `1 ft = 12 in` → `12 in / 1 ft = 1`.
> One foot is twelve inches. So twelve inches over one foot is equal to one. Multiplying by it changes the units but not the amount.
*Screen:* `5 ft × (12 in / 1 ft) = 60 in`; the ft units cancel with a strike.
> Five feet times twelve inches over one foot. The feet cancel like a common factor, and we get sixty inches.
> How do you decide which unit goes on top of the conversion factor?

> Put the unit you want to cancel on the opposite side from where it is now. Feet were on top, so feet go on the bottom.

#### S4 · Units check formulas
*Screen:* "distance = rate × time": `mi/h × h = mi` ✓; `mi/h ÷ h = mi/h²` ✗.
> Units also catch mistakes. Rate times time gives miles per hour times hours, which is miles. Good. If you divided instead, you'd get miles per hour squared, which isn't a distance at all.
