## 6.1 · Scatter plots and association
- **Video:** `u06-l01-scatter-plots`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 2:48 (speech 1:53 · pauses 42 s · 282 words · 4 scenes) · short

**Objective.** Ana makes and reads scatter plots, and describes the association between two
variables by direction (positive, negative, none), form (linear or not) and strength, noting
outliers.

**Topics.**
- Two numbers per person or thing → a point.
- Positive, negative, no association.
- Linear vs. nonlinear form.
- Strong vs. weak; outliers.

#### S1 · Study time and scores
*Screen:* a table of 10 students: hours studied, test score.
> Ten students recorded how many hours they studied and the score they got on a test. Each student gives us two numbers.
*Screen:* each row becomes a dot on axes "hours" and "score".
> Put hours on the horizontal axis and score on the vertical axis, and every student becomes a point. This is a scatter plot.
> Before I describe it, what do you notice? Is there a pattern?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the scatter plot, rising from lower left to upper right; countdown ring.

#### S2 · Direction
*Screen:* the plot with an arrow along the trend.
> As hours go up, scores tend to go up. When larger values of one variable go with larger values of the other, the association is positive.
*Screen:* three mini plots: positive · negative (car age vs. value) · none (shoe size vs. test score).
> When larger values go with smaller ones, like a car's age and its value, the association is negative. And when there's no pattern, like shoe size and test score, there's no association.
> Notice the word tend. Does a positive association mean every student who studied more scored higher?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* two specific points: one who studied 4 hours scored lower than one who studied 2; countdown ring.
> No. It describes the overall trend, not every pair of points.

#### S3 · Form and strength
*Screen:* a curved pattern (temperature vs. ice cream sales peaking then dropping).
> Next, the form. If the points cluster around a straight line, the association is linear. If they follow a curve, it's nonlinear.
*Screen:* two linear plots: tight cluster (strong) · loose cloud (weak).
> Then strength. Points packed tightly around the pattern show a strong association. Points loosely scattered show a weak one.
*Screen:* one point far from the rest is circled: "outlier".
> A point far away from the overall pattern is an outlier. It's worth checking: it might be an error, or a genuinely unusual case.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* scatter plot of "outside temperature (°F)" vs. "heating bill ($)", with points falling from upper left to lower right, tightly along a line, and one point at (70, 190).
> Your turn. Describe the association between outside temperature and heating bill in this scatter plot: direction, form, strength, and any outlier. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* "negative · linear · strong · outlier at (70, 190)".
> It's a strong, negative, linear association: warmer months have lower heating bills. The point at seventy degrees with a one hundred ninety dollar bill is an outlier.
> Remember: describe a scatter plot by its direction, its form, its strength, and any outliers.

## 6.2 · Line of best fit and residuals
- **Video:** `u06-l02-best-fit-and-residuals`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:31 (speech 2:33 · pauses 44 s · 382 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana fits a line to data (by eye and with technology), uses it to predict,
computes residuals as actual minus predicted, and judges a linear model from a residual plot.

**Topics.**
- A line that summarizes the trend.
- Predictions: interpolation vs. extrapolation.
- Residual = actual − predicted.
- Least squares idea.
- Residual plots: random scatter good, a pattern bad.

#### S1 · Summarizing the trend
*Screen:* scatter of arm span vs. height (cm) for 12 students.
> Here are the arm spans and heights of twelve students. The pattern is strong, positive and linear.
*Screen:* a line drawn through the middle of the cloud: `ŷ = 0.95x + 9`.
> A line of best fit runs through the middle of the points, with about as many above as below. Technology finds it for us: predicted height equals zero point nine five times arm span plus nine.
*Screen:* arm span 160 → `0.95(160) + 9 = 161`.
> For an arm span of one hundred sixty centimeters, the line predicts a height of one hundred sixty-one.
> Would you trust this line to predict the height of someone with an arm span of three meters? Why or why not?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the line extended far to the right, off the data; countdown ring.
> Predicting inside the range of the data is called interpolation, and it's fairly safe. Predicting far outside it is extrapolation, and it's risky: the pattern may not continue.

#### S2 · Residuals
*Screen:* one student: arm span 150, actual height 158; predicted `0.95(150) + 9 = 151.5`; a vertical segment from the point to the line.
> No line goes through every point. Take a student with an arm span of one hundred fifty. The line predicts one hundred fifty-one point five, but she's actually one hundred fifty-eight.
*Screen:* `residual = actual − predicted = 158 − 151.5 = 6.5`.
> The difference, actual minus predicted, is the residual. Here it's six point five. A positive residual means the point is above the line. A negative one means it's below.
*Screen:* all vertical segments appear; their squares drawn as little squares.
> The best fit line, technically called the least squares line, is the one that makes the residuals as small as possible overall: it minimizes the total area of these squares.

#### S3 · Residual plots
*Screen:* residual plot: residuals vs. arm span, scattered randomly above and below zero.
> To check if a line is a good model, plot every residual against x. If the residuals look like random scatter around zero, a line fits well.
*Screen:* another data set with a curved scatter; its residual plot shows a U shape.
> Here's a different data set. Its residual plot makes a U shape. What does that pattern tell you about the line?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the U-shaped residual plot; countdown ring.
> The line misses in a systematic way: too high in the middle, too low at the ends. The data is curved, so a line is the wrong model.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* model `ŷ = 2.5x + 40` (minutes of practice x, words per minute typed ŷ). A student practiced 12 minutes and types 74 wpm.
> Your turn. A model predicts typing speed as two point five times minutes of practice, plus forty. A student practiced twelve minutes and types seventy-four words per minute. Find her residual and say what it means. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* predicted `2.5(12) + 40 = 70`; residual `74 − 70 = 4` → "4 wpm faster than predicted".
> The prediction is seventy. Her residual is seventy-four minus seventy, four. She types four words per minute faster than the model predicts.
> Remember: a best fit line summarizes the trend, a residual is actual minus predicted, and a residual plot with no pattern means the line fits.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 6.3 · Interpreting a linear model
- **Video:** `u06-l03-interpreting-models`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 2:49 (speech 1:54 · pauses 44 s · 284 words · 4 scenes) · short

**Objective.** Ana interprets the slope and intercept of a fitted linear model in context,
using the language of prediction ("predicted", "on average"), and decides whether the
intercept is meaningful.

**Topics.**
- Slope: predicted change in y for each one-unit increase in x.
- Intercept: predicted y when x = 0.
- Why "predicted" and "on average".
- Meaningless intercepts.

#### S1 · A used-car model
*Screen:* scatter of car age (years) vs. price ($1000s); line `ŷ = −1.8x + 24`.
> For used cars of one model, a line of best fit is predicted price equals negative one point eight times age, plus twenty-four, in thousands of dollars.
> Say in your own words what negative one point eight means here.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the slope glows; countdown ring.
*Screen:* "For each additional year of age, the predicted price drops by about $1,800."
> For each additional year of age, the predicted price drops by about one thousand eight hundred dollars.

#### S2 · Why "predicted"
*Screen:* two 3-year-old cars at different prices around the line.
> We say predicted, or on average, because real cars don't sit exactly on the line. Two three-year-old cars might sell for different prices. The slope describes the trend, not a promise for every car.
*Screen:* the template: "For each one-unit increase in x, the predicted y changes by slope units."

#### S3 · The intercept
*Screen:* the line meets the y-axis at 24.
> The intercept, twenty-four, is the predicted price of a car that is zero years old: about twenty-four thousand dollars, roughly the price new. Here it makes sense.
*Screen:* a second model: shoe length (cm) vs. height: `ŷ = 4.1x + 62`; data from 22 to 30 cm.
> In another model, height equals four point one times shoe length plus sixty-two. What would the intercept mean? Does it make sense?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the line extended to shoe length 0; countdown ring.
> It would be the height of a person with feet zero centimeters long. That's not a real situation, and it's far outside the data. Here the intercept just positions the line. It has no real meaning.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* model: `ŷ = 0.6x + 12`, x = hours of sunshine per week, ŷ = height of a tomato plant (cm) after a month.
> Your turn. A model predicts a tomato plant's height after a month as zero point six times hours of weekly sunshine, plus twelve centimeters. Interpret the slope and the intercept. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* slope: "each extra hour of weekly sun → about 0.6 cm taller, on average" · intercept: "predicted 12 cm with no sun; only meaningful if 0 hours was in the data".
> Each extra hour of sunshine a week goes with a predicted increase of zero point six centimeters. The intercept predicts twelve centimeters with no sunshine at all, which only makes sense if plants with almost no sun were measured.
> Remember: slope is the predicted change per unit of x, the intercept is the prediction at x equals zero, and both are about the trend, not each individual.

## 6.4 · The correlation coefficient
- **Video:** `u06-l04-correlation-coefficient`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 2:55 (speech 2:06 · pauses 37 s · 314 words · 4 scenes) · short

**Objective.** Ana reads the correlation coefficient r as a measure of the direction and
strength of a linear association, estimates r from a scatter plot, and knows its limits
(only linear, sensitive to outliers).

**Topics.**
- r is between −1 and 1.
- Sign = direction; closeness to ±1 = strength.
- Estimating r from pictures.
- r only measures linear patterns; outliers change it.

#### S1 · One number for strength
*Screen:* five scatter plots in a row, from a tight falling line to a cloud to a tight rising line.
> Words like strong and weak are useful, but sometimes we want a number. The correlation coefficient, r, measures how closely points follow a straight line.
*Screen:* a slider from −1 to 1 under the plots: −1, −0.7, 0, 0.7, 1.
> R is always between negative one and one. Its sign gives the direction: positive for rising, negative for falling. Its size gives the strength: close to one or negative one means the points are almost on a line, close to zero means there's no linear pattern.
> Which is stronger: r equals negative zero point nine, or r equals zero point six?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* both values; countdown ring.
> Negative zero point nine. The sign is only direction. Strength is how close r is to one or negative one.

#### S2 · Guess the r
*Screen:* three plots labeled A, B, C; options 0.95, −0.4, 0.1.
> Match each scatter plot with its r: zero point nine five, negative zero point four, and zero point one.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* A tight rising → 0.95 · B loose falling → −0.4 · C shapeless → 0.1.
> The tight rising line is zero point nine five. The loose, slightly falling cloud is negative zero point four. The shapeless cloud is zero point one.

#### S3 · What r can't see
*Screen:* a perfect parabola of points; r ≈ 0.
> Here's a warning. These points follow a perfect curve, a parabola. But r is about zero.
> R only measures linear association. A strong curved pattern can have r near zero. Always look at the scatter plot, not just the number.
*Screen:* a cloud with r = 0.1; one far outlier added at the top right → r jumps to 0.7.
> And r is sensitive to outliers. Adding one extreme point to a shapeless cloud can push r from zero point one up to zero point seven.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* "A study finds r = −0.85 between hours of screen time at night and hours of sleep." (a) direction (b) strength (c) does r alone prove the relationship is linear?
> Your turn. A study finds r equals negative zero point eight five between screen time at night and hours of sleep. Describe the direction and strength, and say whether r alone proves the pattern is linear. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 15s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* negative · strong · "check the scatter plot: r doesn't prove the form".
> It's a strong negative association: more screen time goes with less sleep. But r alone doesn't prove the form; you'd still check the scatter plot.
> Remember: r is between negative one and one, its sign is direction, its size is linear strength, and you always look at the graph.

## 6.5 · Correlation is not causation
- **Video:** `u06-l05-correlation-not-causation`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:00 (speech 2:02 · pauses 44 s · 306 words · 4 scenes) · short

**Objective.** Ana distinguishes correlation from causation, identifies lurking (confounding)
variables and reverse causation, and knows that controlled experiments are what establish
cause.

**Topics.**
- A strong correlation does not prove cause.
- Lurking variables (ice cream and sunburns).
- Reverse causation and coincidence.
- Experiments with random assignment.

#### S1 · Ice cream and sunburns
*Screen:* scatter: monthly ice cream sales vs. sunburn cases; r = 0.92.
> In one town, months with more ice cream sales also have more sunburns. The correlation is zero point nine two.
> Does eating ice cream cause sunburns? Think about it before I answer.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the scatter plot; countdown ring.
*Screen:* a diagram: "hot, sunny weather" with arrows to both "ice cream" and "sunburn".
> Of course not. Hot, sunny weather causes both. People buy more ice cream and spend more time in the sun. Weather is a lurking variable: something not in the data that drives both.

#### S2 · Other traps
*Screen:* "Cities with more firefighters have more fire damage."
> Cities with more firefighters have more fire damage. Do firefighters cause damage? No: bigger cities have more fires and more firefighters. City size is lurking again.
*Screen:* "People who use more sunscreen have more skin checks." Arrow from checks → sunscreen.
> Sometimes the cause runs backwards. People who get skin checks often are told to use more sunscreen. That's reverse causation.
*Screen:* a chart of two unrelated quantities rising together over years.
> And sometimes it's just coincidence. Two things that both grow over time will correlate, even with no connection at all.

#### S3 · How we find causes
*Screen:* an experiment diagram: 100 volunteers → randomly split → group A (new study app), group B (no app) → compare scores.
> So how do scientists show something causes something else? With a controlled experiment. Volunteers are randomly split into groups. One group gets the treatment, the other doesn't, and everything else is kept the same.
> Why is the random split so important?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the coin flip assigning people; countdown ring.
> Random assignment spreads lurking variables, like motivation or sleep, evenly across both groups. So if the groups end up different, the treatment is the likely reason.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* "Students who eat breakfast have higher grades (r = 0.6). A headline says: 'Breakfast makes you smarter!'"
> Your turn. Students who eat breakfast have higher grades, with r equal to zero point six. A headline says breakfast makes you smarter. Name a possible lurking variable, and describe how you could test the claim. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* lurking: family routines, sleep, income · test: randomly assign students to eat or skip breakfast for a period and compare.
> Possible lurking variables include sleep, family routines, or how organized a household is. To test it, randomly assign students to eat breakfast or not for a few weeks, and compare their grades.
> Remember: correlation shows that two things move together. Only a well-designed experiment shows that one causes the other.
> That's the end of unit six. Next, exponents and radicals.
