## 3.1 · Rate of change and slope
- **Video:** `u03-l01-rate-of-change-and-slope`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:34 (speech 2:34 · pauses 42 s · 384 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana computes a rate of change from a table, a graph or two points, recognizes
that a constant rate means a line, and reads the sign and size of a slope (positive,
negative, zero, undefined).

**Topics.**
- Rate of change = change in output ÷ change in input, with units.
- Constant rate ⇔ straight line.
- Slope formula `m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁)`; rise over run.
- Positive, negative, zero and undefined slopes.

#### S1 · How fast?
*Screen:* a table: hours 0, 1, 2, 3 · miles 0, 55, 110, 165.
> A car's trip is shown in this table. How fast is it going?
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* arrows between rows: +1 hour, +55 miles.
> Every hour, the distance grows by fifty-five miles. That's fifty-five miles per hour, a rate of change.
*Screen:* `rate of change = change in output / change in input = 55 mi / 1 h`.
> A rate of change is the change in the output divided by the change in the input. Its units tell the story: miles per hour.

#### S2 · Constant rate, straight line
*Screen:* the table plotted; points join in a straight line; a staircase of triangles, each 1 right and 55 up.
> When the rate of change is the same between every pair of points, the graph is a straight line. Each step to the right climbs by the same amount.
*Screen:* a second table with changes 10, 20, 40; its graph curves.
> If the rate changes, the graph bends.
> Look at the staircase again. Why must a constant rate give a straight line and not a curve?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the staircase of identical triangles; countdown ring.
> Because every step is the same shape. Identical steps can only line up in one straight direction.

#### S3 · Slope from two points
*Screen:* points (1, 3) and (4, 9) on a grid; a right triangle: run 3, rise 6.
> For a line, the rate of change is called the slope, written m. From one point to another, the slope is the rise over the run.
*Screen:* `m = (9 − 3)/(4 − 1) = 6/3 = 2`.
> From one, three to four, nine, the rise is nine minus three, which is six. The run is four minus one, which is three. Six over three is two.
*Screen:* `m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁)`.
> In general, the slope is the difference of the y values over the difference of the x values, in the same order.
> Would you get the same slope if you started from the other point? Try it in your head.
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `(3 − 9)/(1 − 4)`; countdown ring.
> Yes. Negative six over negative three is still two. What matters is using the same order on top and bottom.

#### S4 · Four kinds of slope
*Screen:* four small graphs: rising (m > 0), falling (m < 0), horizontal (m = 0), vertical (undefined).
> A line going up from left to right has a positive slope. Going down, a negative slope.
> A horizontal line has zero rise, so its slope is zero.
> A vertical line has zero run. Dividing by zero is impossible, so its slope is undefined.
*Screen:* slopes 1/3, 1, 4 drawn from the same point.
> The bigger the absolute value of the slope, the steeper the line.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* points (−2, 5) and (3, −5).
> Your turn. Find the slope of the line through negative two, five and three, negative five, and say whether the line rises or falls. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 15s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `m = (−5 − 5)/(3 − (−2)) = −10/5 = −2`; the line falls.
> Negative five minus five is negative ten. Three minus negative two is five. The slope is negative two. The line falls two units for every unit to the right.
> Remember: slope is rise over run, the same everywhere on a line, and its sign tells you which way the line goes.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 3.2 · Slope-intercept form
- **Video:** `u03-l02-slope-intercept-form`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:22 (speech 2:19 · pauses 47 s · 347 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana reads the slope and y-intercept from `y = mx + b`, graphs a line from them,
writes the equation of a graphed line, and explains why `b` is the y-intercept.

**Topics.**
- `y = mx + b`: m slope, b y-intercept.
- Why b is where the line meets the y-axis (x = 0).
- Graphing: start at b, step by the slope.
- Rewriting an equation into this form.

#### S1 · A starting value and a rate
*Screen:* "A pool has 4 feet of water and fills at 2 feet per hour." `y = 2x + 4`.
> A pool has four feet of water and fills at two feet per hour. After x hours, the depth is y equals two x plus four.
*Screen:* `y = mx + b`, m in blue "rate (slope)", b in green "start (y-intercept)".
> This is slope-intercept form: y equals m x plus b. M is the slope, the rate. B is the starting value.
> Why is b where the line crosses the y axis? Think about which x value every point on the y axis has.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the y-axis glows; countdown ring.
*Screen:* x = 0 → `y = m · 0 + b = b`.
> Every point on the y axis has x equal to zero. Put zero in for x and m times zero disappears. Y equals b.

#### S2 · Graphing from the equation
*Screen:* `y = −(2/3)x + 3`; a dot at (0, 3); then a step down 2, right 3 to (3, 1); the line drawn.
> To graph y equals negative two thirds x plus three, start at the y intercept, zero, three. The slope negative two thirds means down two, right three. That lands on three, one. Draw the line through both points.
*Screen:* a third step to (6, −1).
> One more step to check: down two, right three again, to six, negative one.

#### S3 · From a graph to an equation
*Screen:* a line crossing the y-axis at −1 and passing (2, 5).
> Now the reverse. This line crosses the y axis at negative one. From there to two, five, it rises six and runs two.
> What is its equation?
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `m = 6/2 = 3`, `b = −1` → `y = 3x − 1`.
> The slope is six over two, which is three. The equation is y equals three x minus one.

#### S4 · Rewriting into the form
*Screen:* `4x + 2y = 10`.
> Sometimes the equation comes in another shape, like four x plus two y equals ten. Solve it for y.
*Screen:* `2y = −4x + 10` → `y = −2x + 5`.
> Subtract four x: two y equals negative four x plus ten. Divide by two: y equals negative two x plus five. The slope is negative two, the y intercept five.
> In y equals five, what are m and b? What does that line look like?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `y = 5`; countdown ring.
> M is zero and b is five. It's a horizontal line through five.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* `3x − y = 2`.
> Your turn. Write three x minus y equals two in slope-intercept form, name the slope and the intercept, and describe how to graph it. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `y = 3x − 2`; graph: start (0, −2), up 3 right 1.
> Subtract three x and multiply by negative one: y equals three x minus two. Start at zero, negative two, and go up three, right one.
> Remember: in y equals m x plus b, b is where you start and m is how you move.

## 3.3 · Point-slope form and standard form
- **Video:** `u03-l03-point-slope-and-standard-form`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:43 (speech 2:46 · pauses 42 s · 414 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana writes a line in point-slope form from a point and a slope, converts
between point-slope, slope-intercept and standard form, and uses standard form to find
intercepts quickly.

**Topics.**
- Point-slope form `y − y₁ = m(x − x₁)` comes from the slope formula.
- Using it with any point on the line.
- Standard form `Ax + By = C`.
- Intercepts from standard form (cover-up method).
- Converting between forms.

#### S1 · A point and a slope
*Screen:* a line through (2, 3) with slope 4; the y-intercept is off the screen.
> Suppose you know a line has slope four and passes through two, three, but you don't know where it crosses the y axis. Is there an equation you can write right away?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* a general point (x, y) on the line; `(y − 3)/(x − 2) = 4`.
> Take any other point x, y on the line. The slope between it and two, three must be four. So y minus three, over x minus two, equals four.
*Screen:* multiply by `(x − 2)`: `y − 3 = 4(x − 2)`.
> Multiply both sides by x minus two, and you get y minus three equals four times the quantity x minus two. That is point-slope form.
*Screen:* `y − y₁ = m(x − x₁)`, with `(x₁, y₁)` labeled "a point you know".
> In general, y minus y one equals m times the quantity x minus x one, where x one, y one is any point you know.

#### S2 · Watch the signs
*Screen:* slope −3 through (−1, 4): `y − 4 = −3(x − (−1))` → `y − 4 = −3(x + 1)`.
> With negative coordinates, be careful. Slope negative three through negative one, four: y minus four equals negative three times the quantity x minus negative one, which becomes x plus one.
*Screen:* `y − 4 = −3x − 3` → `y = −3x + 1`.
> Distribute and add four, and you get slope-intercept form: y equals negative three x plus one.

#### S3 · Standard form
*Screen:* `2x + 3y = 12`.
> Lines are also written in standard form: A x plus B y equals C, with whole numbers and x first. For example, two x plus three y equals twelve.
*Screen:* cover-up: x = 0 → `3y = 12` → y-int (0, 4); y = 0 → `2x = 12` → x-int (6, 0); line through both.
> Standard form makes intercepts easy. Cover up the x term, as if x were zero: three y equals twelve, so y is four. Cover up the y term: two x equals twelve, so x is six. Draw the line through zero, four and six, zero.
> Why does covering up a term give an intercept? What value does that variable have there?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the two intercepts glow; countdown ring.
> On the y axis, x is zero, so the x term vanishes. On the x axis, y is zero. Covering the term is just setting that variable to zero.

#### S4 · Converting
*Screen:* `y = (2/3)x − 5` → multiply by 3 → `3y = 2x − 15` → `−2x + 3y = −15` → `2x − 3y = 15`.
> To change y equals two thirds x minus five into standard form, clear the fraction: multiply by three. Three y equals two x minus fifteen. Move the x term left, and make the x coefficient positive: two x minus three y equals fifteen.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* "A line through (4, −1) with slope 1/2."
> Your turn. Write the line through four, negative one with slope one half in point-slope form, then in slope-intercept form, then in standard form. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `y + 1 = ½(x − 4)` → `y = ½x − 3` → `x − 2y = 6`.
> Point-slope: y plus one equals one half times the quantity x minus four. Slope-intercept: y equals one half x minus three. Standard: x minus two y equals six.
> Remember: point-slope is for a point and a slope, slope-intercept shows the start and the rate, and standard form gives the intercepts fast. They're three ways of writing the same line.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 3.4 · Writing linear equations
- **Video:** `u03-l04-writing-linear-equations`

**Objective.** Ana writes the equation of a line from a graph, from two points, from a table,
and from a verbal description, choosing the most convenient form each time.

**Topics.**
- The two things every line needs: a slope and a point.
- From a graph.
- From two points (slope, then point-slope).
- From a table (constant rate; starting value).
- From a description (rate and starting amount).

#### S1 · Two ingredients
*Screen:* recipe card "Line = slope + one point".
> Every method for writing a line comes down to two ingredients: a slope, and one point. Once you have those, you can write the equation.
> Can you think of a reason why one point alone isn't enough?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* a single dot with many lines spinning through it; countdown ring.
> Through one point there are infinitely many lines, one for every direction. The slope picks the direction.

#### S2 · From a graph
*Screen:* a line through (0, 2) and (3, −4).
> From a graph, look for points where the line crosses grid corners. This one passes zero, two and three, negative four. The rise is negative six and the run three, so the slope is negative two. The y intercept is two.
*Screen:* `y = −2x + 2`.
> Y equals negative two x plus two.

#### S3 · From two points
*Screen:* points (−3, 1) and (5, 7).
> From two points, first find the slope, then use either point.
*Screen:* `m = (7 − 1)/(5 + 3) = 6/8 = 3/4`; `y − 7 = ¾(x − 5)` → `y = ¾x + 13/4`.
> Seven minus one is six, five minus negative three is eight, so the slope is three fourths. With the point five, seven: y minus seven equals three fourths times the quantity x minus five. In slope-intercept form, y equals three fourths x plus thirteen fourths.
*Screen:* check (−3, 1): `¾(−3) + 13/4 = −9/4 + 13/4 = 1` ✓.
> Check with the other point: three fourths of negative three plus thirteen fourths is one. It works.

#### S4 · From a table
*Screen:* x: 2, 4, 6, 8 · y: 11, 17, 23, 29.
> Here's a table. What is its equation? Start by checking that the rate is constant.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the table; countdown ring.
*Screen:* +2 in x, +6 in y → m = 3; work backwards to x = 0: y = 5 → `y = 3x + 5`.
> X goes up by two while y goes up by six, so the slope is three. To find the start, go back from x equals two to x equals zero: two steps of negative one in x, so subtract six from eleven. Five. The equation is y equals three x plus five.

#### S5 · From a description
*Screen:* "A candle is 30 cm tall and burns 2.5 cm per hour."
> A candle is thirty centimeters tall and burns two point five centimeters per hour. The starting value is thirty, the rate is negative two point five.
*Screen:* `h = −2.5t + 30`.
> H equals negative two point five t plus thirty. The rate is negative, because the candle gets shorter.

#### S6 · Your turn
*Screen:* "A taxi charged $13 for a 4-mile ride and $22 for a 10-mile ride. The cost is linear. Write the equation and give the meaning of the slope and the intercept."
> Your turn. A taxi charged thirteen dollars for four miles and twenty-two dollars for ten miles. Write the cost as a linear function of miles, and say what the slope and intercept mean. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `m = 9/6 = 1.5`; `C = 1.5d + 7`: $1.50 per mile, $7 base fare.
> The cost went up nine dollars over six miles: a dollar fifty per mile. Thirteen minus four times one fifty is seven. C equals one point five d plus seven: seven dollars to start, plus a dollar fifty per mile.
> Remember: whatever you're given, find a slope and a point, then write the line.

## 3.5 · Parallel and perpendicular lines
- **Video:** `u03-l05-parallel-and-perpendicular`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:15 (speech 2:19 · pauses 42 s · 347 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana identifies parallel lines (equal slopes) and perpendicular lines
(slopes that are opposite reciprocals), justifies the perpendicular rule with a rotated
triangle, and writes lines parallel or perpendicular to a given line through a point.

**Topics.**
- Parallel: same slope, different intercept.
- Perpendicular: product of slopes is −1.
- Why: rotating the slope triangle 90°.
- Writing a parallel or perpendicular line through a point.

#### S1 · Train tracks
*Screen:* two rails; `y = 2x + 1` and `y = 2x − 3` on a grid.
> Train rails never meet. Two lines that never meet are parallel. Look at y equals two x plus one and y equals two x minus three.
> What do they have in common, and what's different?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* both equations; countdown ring.
> Same slope, two. Different intercepts. Same slope means the same steepness and direction, so the gap between them never changes.

#### S2 · Perpendicular
*Screen:* a slope triangle for m = 2/3 (right 3, up 2); it rotates 90° to become right −2, up 3, i.e. slope −3/2.
> Perpendicular lines meet at a right angle. Take a line with slope two thirds and its slope triangle: right three, up two. Rotate the whole picture a quarter turn.
> The run becomes the rise, and the rise becomes a run in the other direction: up three, left two. The new slope is negative three halves.
*Screen:* `2/3 · (−3/2) = −1`.
> Flip the fraction and change the sign. That's called the opposite reciprocal, and the two slopes multiply to negative one.
> What happens with a horizontal line? Can you use this rule with it?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* y = 2 and x = 4 crossing; countdown ring.
> A horizontal line has slope zero, and zero has no reciprocal. The perpendicular to a horizontal line is vertical, with an undefined slope. That pair is the one exception to the rule.

#### S3 · Writing them
*Screen:* "Line through (6, 5), parallel to `y = −½x + 4`."
> To write a line through six, five that is parallel to y equals negative one half x plus four, keep the slope, negative one half, and use the new point.
*Screen:* `y − 5 = −½(x − 6)` → `y = −½x + 8`; both lines drawn, the gap between them constant.
> Y minus five equals negative one half times the quantity x minus six. That simplifies to y equals negative one half x plus eight. Same slope, new intercept.
*Screen:* "Line through (6, 5), perpendicular to `y = −½x + 4`": `m = 2` → `y − 5 = 2(x − 6)` → `y = 2x − 7`; a right-angle mark where the lines meet.
> For the perpendicular line, flip the slope and change its sign: negative one half becomes two. Y minus five equals two times the quantity x minus six. Y equals two x minus seven.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* `3x + 4y = 8` and the point (−3, 2).
> Your turn. Write the line through negative three, two that is perpendicular to three x plus four y equals eight. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `y = −¾x + 2` → perpendicular slope `4/3` → `y − 2 = 4/3(x + 3)` → `y = 4/3x + 6`.
> First find the slope: solving for y gives negative three fourths. The perpendicular slope is four thirds. Y minus two equals four thirds times the quantity x plus three, which is y equals four thirds x plus six.
> Remember: parallel lines share a slope, perpendicular slopes are opposite reciprocals.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 3.6 · Slope and intercept in context
- **Video:** `u03-l06-slope-intercept-in-context`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 2:53 (speech 2:00 · pauses 42 s · 299 words · 4 scenes) · short

**Objective.** Ana states what the slope and the y-intercept of a linear model mean in a
real situation, with units, and recognizes when an intercept has no real meaning.

**Topics.**
- Slope = rate with units ("per").
- Intercept = value when the input is zero.
- Sentences that interpret them.
- Intercepts outside the sensible domain.

#### S1 · A savings plan
*Screen:* `S = 25w + 120`: savings in dollars after w weeks.
> Ana's savings after w weeks are S equals twenty-five w plus one hundred twenty dollars. What do the twenty-five and the one hundred twenty mean?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the two numbers glow; countdown ring.
*Screen:* "slope: +$25 per week" · "intercept: $120 at week 0".
> Twenty-five is the slope: her savings grow by twenty-five dollars per week. One hundred twenty is the y intercept: when w is zero, at the start, she has one hundred twenty dollars.

#### S2 · Units make sentences
*Screen:* template: "For every 1 [input unit], [output] changes by [slope] [output units]."
> The units of the slope are output units per input unit. That gives a sentence template: for every one input unit, the output changes by the slope.
*Screen:* `T = −6.5h + 15`: temperature in °C at altitude h km.
> The temperature at altitude h kilometers is T equals negative six point five h plus fifteen. For every kilometer up, the temperature drops six point five degrees. At ground level, it is fifteen degrees.

#### S3 · When the intercept makes no sense
*Screen:* `W = 3.2L − 40`: a fish's weight in grams for length L in cm; data from 20 to 50 cm.
> A scientist models a fish's weight as W equals three point two L minus forty grams, using fish from twenty to fifty centimeters long.
> What would the intercept, negative forty, mean? Does it make sense?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the scatter of fish data and the line extended to L = 0; countdown ring.
> It would be the weight of a fish of length zero: negative forty grams. That's impossible. The model was only built from fish twenty to fifty centimeters long, so it shouldn't be used near zero. An intercept only has meaning if the input zero makes sense.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* `P = −0.8t + 64`: percent of a phone battery after t minutes of a game.
> Your turn. A phone's battery is P equals negative zero point eight t plus sixty-four percent after t minutes of a game. Interpret the slope and the intercept in sentences, and find when the battery dies. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* "loses 0.8% per minute" · "64% when the game starts" · `0 = −0.8t + 64` → t = 80 min.
> The battery loses zero point eight percent per minute. It had sixty-four percent when the game started. It dies when P is zero: t equals eighty minutes.
> Remember: say the slope as a rate with "per", say the intercept as the value at the start, and ask whether the start makes sense.

## 3.7 · Solving f(x) = g(x) with graphs and tables

**Objective.** Ana solves `f(x) = g(x)` by finding where two graphs intersect or where a
table shows equal outputs, explains why the x-coordinate is the solution, and checks it
algebraically.

**Topics.**
- An equation as two functions.
- The intersection point; its x is the solution.
- Using a table: equal outputs.
- Approximate vs. exact; checking algebraically.

#### S1 · Two phone plans
*Screen:* `f(x) = 30 + 0.10x` and `g(x) = 0.25x` (cost for x texts).
> Plan F costs thirty dollars plus ten cents per text. Plan G costs twenty-five cents per text. When do they cost the same?
*Screen:* `30 + 0.10x = 0.25x` → this is `f(x) = g(x)`.
> That's the equation f of x equals g of x. We can solve it with algebra, but also with a graph or a table.

#### S2 · The intersection
*Screen:* both lines graphed; they cross at (200, 50).
> Graph both functions. Where do the lines cross?
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
> At two hundred, fifty. At two hundred texts, both plans cost fifty dollars.
> Which number is the solution of the equation, two hundred or fifty? Why?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the intersection point with both coordinates highlighted; countdown ring.
> The solution is x equals two hundred. The equation asks for the input that makes the outputs equal. Fifty is the equal output, the cost they share.

#### S3 · With a table
*Screen:* table x: 0, 100, 200, 300 · f: 30, 40, 50, 60 · g: 0, 25, 50, 75; the row 200 glows.
> A table shows the same thing. Look for the row where the two output columns match. At two hundred, both are fifty.
*Screen:* before 200, f > g; after 200, g > f.
> Notice what happens around it: before two hundred, plan G is cheaper; after, plan F is cheaper. The solution is where they switch.

#### S4 · When it's not on the grid
*Screen:* `f(x) = 2x + 1`, `g(x) = −x + 5`; crossing near (1.3, 3.7).
> Sometimes the lines cross between grid marks. Here the crossing is near x equals one point three. A graph gives an estimate.
> Is it one point three exactly? What could you do to be sure?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* zoom into the crossing; it still sits between grid lines; countdown ring.
*Screen:* `2x + 1 = −x + 5` → `3x = 4` → `x = 4/3`.
> Algebra gives the exact answer: two x plus one equals negative x plus five, so x equals four thirds.

#### S5 · Your turn

> Remember: f of x equals g of x is solved where the graphs cross, and the answer is the x coordinate of that point.
> That's the end of linear functions. Next, sequences.
