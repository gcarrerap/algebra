## 9.1 · Polynomial vocabulary
- **Video:** `u09-l01-polynomial-vocabulary`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:04 (speech 2:08 · pauses 42 s · 320 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana identifies polynomials, writes them in standard form, and names their
degree, leading coefficient and type (monomial, binomial, trinomial; linear, quadratic,
cubic).

**Topics.**
- Monomials: a number times variables with whole-number exponents.
- Polynomial = sum of monomials; what is not a polynomial.
- Degree of a term and of a polynomial.
- Standard form and leading coefficient.
- Names by number of terms and by degree.

#### S1 · Building blocks
*Screen:* `5`, `3x`, `−2x²`, `7xy³` as blocks; `x⁻¹`, `√x`, `2/x` crossed out.
> A monomial is a number, a variable, or a product of numbers and variables with whole-number exponents. Five, three x, negative two x squared, and seven x y cubed are monomials.
> Look at the crossed-out ones: x to the negative one, root x, and two over x. What do they have in common?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the three crossed-out expressions; countdown ring.
> Their exponents aren't whole numbers: x to the negative one, x to the one half, and two times x to the negative one. Monomials only allow exponents zero, one, two, three, and so on.
*Screen:* `4x³ − 2x + 9`.
> A polynomial is a sum of monomials, like four x cubed minus two x plus nine. Each monomial is a term.

#### S2 · Degree and standard form
*Screen:* `3 + 5x² − x⁴ + 2x` with each term's degree under it: 0, 2, 4, 1.
> The degree of a term is its exponent. For a polynomial, the degree is the highest one. Three plus five x squared minus x to the fourth plus two x has degree four.
*Screen:* reordered: `−x⁴ + 5x² + 2x + 3`; the `−1` circled as leading coefficient.
> Standard form writes the terms from highest degree to lowest: negative x to the fourth, plus five x squared, plus two x, plus three. The first coefficient, here negative one, is the leading coefficient.
*Screen:* `6x²y³`: degree 2 + 3 = 5.
> With several variables in one term, add their exponents. Six x squared y cubed has degree five.

#### S3 · Names
*Screen:* table: 1 term monomial · 2 binomial · 3 trinomial · degree 1 linear · 2 quadratic · 3 cubic.
> Polynomials get names by their number of terms: monomial, binomial, trinomial. And by their degree: linear, quadratic, cubic.
*Screen:* `x² − 9`.
> So x squared minus nine is a quadratic binomial.
> What about seven? What's the degree of a constant?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `7 = 7x⁰`; countdown ring.
> Zero. Seven is seven times x to the zero. It's a monomial of degree zero.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* `8 − 3x³ + x` · write in standard form, give the degree, leading coefficient, and name.
> Your turn. For eight minus three x cubed plus x, write it in standard form, and give its degree, leading coefficient, and name. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `−3x³ + x + 8` · degree 3 · leading coefficient −3 · cubic trinomial.
> In standard form, negative three x cubed plus x plus eight. Degree three, leading coefficient negative three. It's a cubic trinomial.
> Remember: polynomials are sums of monomials with whole-number exponents, written from highest degree to lowest, and named by terms and degree.

## 9.2 · Adding and subtracting polynomials
- **Video:** `u09-l02-adding-subtracting-polynomials`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 2:58 (speech 2:02 · pauses 42 s · 306 words · 4 scenes) · short

**Objective.** Ana adds and subtracts polynomials by combining like terms, distributing the
minus sign over every term of the subtracted polynomial, and understands that the result is
always a polynomial (closure).

**Topics.**
- Adding = combining like terms (horizontal and vertical layouts).
- Subtracting = adding the opposite of every term.
- The most common sign error.
- Closure: the result is a polynomial.

#### S1 · Like terms again
*Screen:* `(3x² + 2x − 5) + (x² − 6x + 1)`; like terms tinted: x² blue, x yellow, constants green.
> Adding polynomials is combining like terms, which you already know. In three x squared plus two x minus five, plus x squared minus six x plus one, match the colors.
*Screen:* `4x² − 4x − 4`.
> Three x squared plus x squared is four x squared. Two x minus six x is negative four x. Negative five plus one is negative four.
*Screen:* vertical layout with columns by degree; a missing x term shown as `0x`.
> You can also stack them in columns by degree, like adding numbers. Leave a gap, or write zero, for a missing term.

#### S2 · Subtracting
*Screen:* `(5x² − 3x + 2) − (2x² + 4x − 7)`.
> Subtraction needs care. Five x squared minus three x plus two, minus the quantity two x squared plus four x minus seven.
> The minus sign applies to which terms of the second polynomial?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the minus sign glows; countdown ring.
*Screen:* `−(2x² + 4x − 7) = −2x² − 4x + 7`.
> To all of them. Subtracting the polynomial means adding the opposite of every term: negative two x squared, negative four x, and plus seven.
*Screen:* `5x² − 3x + 2 − 2x² − 4x + 7 = 3x² − 7x + 9`.
> Now combine: three x squared minus seven x plus nine.
*Screen:* the wrong version: `−2x² + 4x − 7` in red → `3x² + x − 5`.
> The classic mistake changes only the first sign. That gives three x squared plus x minus five, which is wrong.

#### S3 · Always a polynomial
*Screen:* "polynomial ± polynomial = polynomial".
> Here's something to think about. When you add or subtract two polynomials, could the answer ever stop being a polynomial?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
> No. Combining like terms only adds coefficients. The exponents stay whole numbers. So the answer is always a polynomial. Mathematicians say polynomials are closed under addition and subtraction, just like whole numbers are closed under addition.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* `(4a³ − a + 6) − (a³ + 2a² − a − 3)`.
> Your turn. Subtract: four a cubed minus a plus six, minus the quantity a cubed plus two a squared minus a minus three. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `4a³ − a + 6 − a³ − 2a² + a + 3 = 3a³ − 2a² + 9`.
> Change every sign of the second polynomial: minus a cubed, minus two a squared, plus a, plus three. Combine: three a cubed minus two a squared plus nine. The a terms cancel.
> Remember: add polynomials by combining like terms, and subtract by changing the sign of every term you're subtracting.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 9.3 · Multiplying polynomials
- **Video:** `u09-l03-multiplying-polynomials`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:15 (speech 2:19 · pauses 42 s · 348 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana multiplies monomials by polynomials, binomials by binomials and larger
polynomials using the distributive property and the area (box) model, and checks the number
of products before combining.

**Topics.**
- Monomial × polynomial (distributive).
- Binomial × binomial with the area model.
- Every term times every term; counting products.
- Binomial × trinomial in a 2 × 3 box.
- Closure under multiplication.

#### S1 · Distributing again
*Screen:* `3x(2x² − 5x + 4) = 6x³ − 15x² + 12x`; arrows from 3x to each term.
> Multiplying a monomial by a polynomial is the distributive property. Three x times two x squared is six x cubed. Times negative five x is negative fifteen x squared. Times four is twelve x.

#### S2 · The area model
*Screen:* a rectangle with sides `(x + 4)` and `(x + 3)`, split into a 2 × 2 grid: `x²`, `3x`, `4x`, `12`.
> For x plus four times x plus three, draw a rectangle with those side lengths. Split each side into its terms. Each box is a product: x times x is x squared, x times three is three x, four times x is four x, four times three is twelve.
*Screen:* `x² + 7x + 12`.
> Add the boxes and combine like terms: x squared plus seven x plus twelve.
> How many products will there be when you multiply a binomial by a binomial? And a binomial by a trinomial?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* an empty 2 × 2 box and an empty 2 × 3 box; countdown ring.
> Two times two is four products. Two times three is six. Every term of one factor meets every term of the other. Counting the boxes before you combine helps you check you didn't miss any.

#### S3 · Signs in the boxes
*Screen:* `(2x − 5)(x + 3)` in a box: `2x²`, `6x`, `−5x`, `−15` → `2x² + x − 15`.
> Negative terms go in the boxes with their signs. For two x minus five times x plus three: two x squared, six x, negative five x, negative fifteen. Combine: two x squared plus x minus fifteen.

#### S4 · Bigger products
*Screen:* `(x + 2)(x² − 3x + 5)` in a 2 × 3 box → `x³ − 3x² + 5x + 2x² − 6x + 10` → `x³ − x² − x + 10`.
> For x plus two times x squared minus three x plus five, use a two by three box. Six products: x cubed, negative three x squared, five x, two x squared, negative six x, and ten. Combine like terms: x cubed minus x squared minus x plus ten.
> What's the degree of the product, and how could you have predicted it before multiplying?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the degrees 1 and 2 of the factors; countdown ring.
> Degree three. The highest-degree terms, x and x squared, multiply to x cubed. Degrees add when you multiply.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* `(3x − 1)(2x² + x − 4)`.
> Your turn. Multiply three x minus one times two x squared plus x minus four. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* box: `6x³, 3x², −12x, −2x², −x, 4` → `6x³ + x² − 13x + 4`.
> The six products are six x cubed, three x squared, negative twelve x, negative two x squared, negative x, and four. Combined: six x cubed plus x squared minus thirteen x plus four.
> Remember: multiply every term by every term, the area model keeps track for you, and then combine like terms.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 9.4 · Special products
- **Video:** `u09-l04-special-products`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:20 (speech 2:23 · pauses 40 s · 358 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana recognizes and expands the square of a sum, the square of a difference,
and the product of a sum and a difference, explaining each with the area model, and uses them
for mental math.

**Topics.**
- `(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²` (and why it isn't `a² + b²`).
- `(a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²`.
- `(a + b)(a − b) = a² − b²`: the middle terms cancel.
- Mental math: `31²`, `49 × 51`.

#### S1 · The missing piece
*Screen:* `(x + 5)² = x² + 25 ?`
> Many people think x plus five, squared, is x squared plus twenty-five. In unit seven we saw that's wrong. Now we can see what's missing.
*Screen:* a square with side x + 5, split into `x²`, `5x`, `5x`, `25`.
> Draw a square with side x plus five. Inside are a big x squared, a small twenty-five, and two rectangles of five x each.
*Screen:* `(x + 5)² = x² + 10x + 25`; the two rectangles glow.
> So x plus five, squared, is x squared plus ten x plus twenty-five. The two rectangles are the missing piece.
*Screen:* `(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²`.
> In general, a plus b squared is a squared plus two a b plus b squared: square the first, twice the product, square the last.

#### S2 · Square of a difference
*Screen:* `(2y − 3)² = 4y² − 12y + 9`.
> A difference works the same, but the middle term is negative. Two y minus three, squared: four y squared, minus twice two y times three, twelve y, plus nine.
> Why is the last term plus nine and not minus nine?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `(−3)²`; countdown ring.
> Because negative three squared is positive nine. Only the middle term carries the minus.

#### S3 · Sum times difference
*Screen:* `(x + 6)(x − 6)` in a box: `x²`, `−6x`, `6x`, `−36` → `x² − 36`.
> Now multiply a sum by a difference of the same two terms: x plus six times x minus six. In the box: x squared, negative six x, six x, negative thirty-six.
> Look at the middle terms. What happens to them?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `−6x` and `6x` glow; countdown ring.
> They cancel. Only x squared minus thirty-six is left. A sum times a difference gives a difference of squares: a plus b times a minus b equals a squared minus b squared.

#### S4 · Mental math
*Screen:* `49 × 51 = (50 − 1)(50 + 1) = 2500 − 1 = 2499`.
> These patterns make mental math fast. Forty-nine times fifty-one is fifty minus one times fifty plus one: two thousand five hundred minus one, two thousand four hundred ninety-nine.
*Screen:* `31² = (30 + 1)² = 900 + 60 + 1 = 961`.
> And thirty-one squared is thirty plus one, squared: nine hundred plus sixty plus one, nine hundred sixty-one.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) `(3a + 4)²` (b) `(5 − x)(5 + x)` (c) `98²` mentally.
> Your turn. Expand three a plus four, squared, and five minus x times five plus x. Then compute ninety-eight squared in your head. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `9a² + 24a + 16` · `25 − x²` · `(100 − 2)² = 10000 − 400 + 4 = 9604`.
> Nine a squared plus twenty-four a plus sixteen. Twenty-five minus x squared. And ninety-eight squared is one hundred minus two, squared: ten thousand minus four hundred plus four, nine thousand six hundred four.
> Remember: a squared binomial has a middle term, twice the product. A sum times a difference has none.
> That's the end of polynomials. Next, factoring.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.
