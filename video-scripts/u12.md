## 12.1 · Solving by factoring
- **Video:** `u12-l01-solving-by-factoring`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 2:57 (speech 2:03 · pauses 42 s · 308 words · 4 scenes) · short

**Objective.** Ana solves quadratic equations by writing them in standard form equal to zero,
factoring, and applying the zero product property, and checks both solutions.

**Topics.**
- Standard form = 0 first.
- Factor (GCF, trinomial, special patterns).
- Zero product property; two, one (double) or no factorable solutions.
- Never divide by the variable.

#### S1 · A garden
*Screen:* a rectangular garden, length 3 m longer than width, area 40 m²: `x(x + 3) = 40`.
> A garden is three meters longer than it is wide, and its area is forty square meters. If the width is x, then x times the quantity x plus three equals forty.
> Could you set x equal to forty and x plus three equal to forty? Why not?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
> No. The zero product property only works when the product is zero. So first, get zero on one side.

#### S2 · Zero, factor, solve
*Screen:* `x² + 3x = 40` → `x² + 3x − 40 = 0` → `(x + 8)(x − 5) = 0` → `x = −8` or `x = 5`.
> Expand and subtract forty: x squared plus three x minus forty equals zero. Factor: x plus eight times x minus five. So x is negative eight or five.
*Screen:* −8 crossed out in context: "a width can't be negative" → garden 5 × 8.
> A width can't be negative, so the garden is five meters wide and eight meters long. Check: five times eight is forty.

#### S3 · Two traps
*Screen:* `x² = 6x` → divide by x → `x = 6` (red: a solution was lost) → correct: `x² − 6x = 0` → `x(x − 6) = 0` → `x = 0, 6`.
> Here's a trap. In x squared equals six x, it's tempting to divide both sides by x and get x equals six. What's wrong with that?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the red path; countdown ring.
> Dividing by x assumes x isn't zero, and zero is a solution: zero squared equals six times zero. Instead, move everything to one side: x times x minus six equals zero, so x is zero or six.
*Screen:* `x² − 10x + 25 = 0` → `(x − 5)² = 0` → `x = 5` (double root); the parabola touches the x-axis.
> And when the trinomial is a perfect square, like x minus five squared equals zero, both factors give five. There's only one solution, called a double root. On the graph, the parabola just touches the x axis.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) `2x² − 18 = 0` (b) `3x² + 7x = 6`.
> Your turn. Solve two x squared minus eighteen equals zero, and three x squared plus seven x equals six. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* (a) `2(x + 3)(x − 3) = 0` → `x = ±3` · (b) `3x² + 7x − 6 = 0` → `(3x − 2)(x + 3) = 0` → `x = 2/3, −3`.
> The first is two times x plus three times x minus three, so x is three or negative three. For the second, subtract six and factor: three x minus two times x plus three, so x is two thirds or negative three.
> Remember: get zero on one side, factor, set each factor to zero, and never divide by the variable.

## 12.2 · Solving with square roots
- **Video:** `u12-l02-square-roots`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:23 (speech 2:21 · pauses 47 s · 352 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana solves equations of the form `ax² + c = 0` and `a(x − h)² = k` by isolating
the square and taking both square roots, simplifying radicals, and recognizes when there is
no real solution.

**Topics.**
- `x² = k`: two solutions `±√k`.
- Isolate the square first.
- Squared binomials: `(x − h)² = k`.
- k negative → no real solution; k = 0 → one.

#### S1 · Two answers
*Screen:* `x² = 49`.
> Solve x squared equals forty-nine.
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `x = 7` and `x = −7`; `x = ±√49 = ±7`.
> Seven works. But so does negative seven, because negative seven squared is also forty-nine. Taking the square root to solve, you must include both: x equals plus or minus seven.
> Wait: we said the square root of forty-nine is only seven. Why do we need the plus or minus here?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* `√49 = 7` next to `x² = 49 → x = ±7`; countdown ring.
> The square root symbol gives one number, the positive one. But the equation asks for every number whose square is forty-nine, and there are two. The plus or minus brings back the one the symbol leaves out.

#### S2 · Isolate the square
*Screen:* `3x² − 5 = 70` → `3x² = 75` → `x² = 25` → `x = ±5`.
> Isolate the squared term first. Three x squared minus five equals seventy. Add five, divide by three: x squared equals twenty-five. So x is plus or minus five.
*Screen:* `x² = 18` → `x = ±√18 = ±3√2 ≈ ±4.24`.
> If the number isn't a perfect square, simplify the radical. X squared equals eighteen gives plus or minus three root two, about plus or minus four point two four.

#### S3 · A squared binomial
*Screen:* `(x − 4)² = 9` → `x − 4 = ±3` → `x = 7` or `x = 1`.
> This works on a squared binomial too. In the quantity x minus four squared equals nine, the quantity x minus four must be plus or minus three. So x is four plus three, seven, or four minus three, one.

#### S4 · No real solution
*Screen:* `x² + 9 = 0` → `x² = −9`.
> What about x squared plus nine equals zero? Then x squared equals negative nine.
> Is there any real number whose square is negative nine?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the parabola `y = x² + 9` above the x-axis; countdown ring.
> No. Squares are never negative. There is no real solution, and the graph of y equals x squared plus nine never touches the x axis.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) `2(x + 1)² = 50` (b) `4x² + 1 = 0` (c) `(x − 3)² = 12`.
> Your turn. Solve two times the quantity x plus one squared equals fifty, four x squared plus one equals zero, and the quantity x minus three squared equals twelve. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* (a) `(x + 1)² = 25` → `x = 4, −6` · (b) `x² = −1/4` → no real solution · (c) `x = 3 ± 2√3`.
> First: x plus one squared is twenty-five, so x plus one is plus or minus five: x is four or negative six. Second: x squared would be negative one fourth, so no real solution. Third: x minus three is plus or minus two root three, so x is three plus or minus two root three.
> Remember: isolate the square, take both square roots, and if the square must be negative, there's no real solution.

## 12.3 · Completing the square
- **Video:** `u12-l03-completing-the-square`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:08 (speech 2:12 · pauses 42 s · 329 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana completes the square with an area model, uses it to solve any quadratic
equation, and rewrites a quadratic in vertex form to find its vertex.

**Topics.**
- The missing corner: `x² + bx + (b/2)²`.
- Solving: move c, complete, take roots.
- When a ≠ 1: divide first.
- Vertex form from completing the square.

#### S1 · The missing corner
*Screen:* tiles: one `x²` square, six `x` strips arranged as two groups of 3 on two sides; a missing corner.
> Here's x squared plus six x with algebra tiles. Split the six x strips in half and place three along each side of the big square.
> What's missing to make a complete square?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the empty corner glows; countdown ring.
*Screen:* a 3 × 3 corner of 9 unit tiles fills in: `x² + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)²`.
> A three by three corner, nine small tiles. Add nine, and you have a perfect square: x plus three, squared.
*Screen:* rule: "half of b, squared": `(6/2)² = 9`.
> The number to add is always half of b, squared.

#### S2 · Solving
*Screen:* `x² + 6x − 7 = 0` → `x² + 6x = 7` → `x² + 6x + 9 = 7 + 9` → `(x + 3)² = 16` → `x + 3 = ±4` → `x = 1, −7`.
> Let's solve x squared plus six x minus seven equals zero. Move the seven over: x squared plus six x equals seven. Add nine to both sides: x plus three squared equals sixteen. Take both roots: x plus three is plus or minus four. X is one or negative seven.
> Why do we add nine to the right side too?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the balance scale; countdown ring.
> To keep the equation balanced. Adding to only one side would change it.

#### S3 · When a isn't one
*Screen:* `2x² − 8x − 10 = 0` → `x² − 4x − 5 = 0` → `x² − 4x + 4 = 5 + 4` → `(x − 2)² = 9` → `x = 5, −1`.
> If the x squared term has a coefficient, divide everything by it first. Two x squared minus eight x minus ten becomes x squared minus four x minus five. Move the five, add four, which is half of negative four squared. X minus two squared equals nine. X is five or negative one.

#### S4 · Vertex form
*Screen:* `y = x² − 10x + 21` → `y = (x² − 10x + 25) − 25 + 21` → `y = (x − 5)² − 4` → vertex (5, −4).
> Completing the square also turns standard form into vertex form. For y equals x squared minus ten x plus twenty-one, add and subtract twenty-five: y equals x minus five squared, minus four. The vertex is five, negative four.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) solve `x² + 8x + 3 = 0` (b) write `y = x² + 2x − 8` in vertex form.
> Your turn. Solve x squared plus eight x plus three equals zero by completing the square, and write y equals x squared plus two x minus eight in vertex form. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* (a) `(x + 4)² = 13` → `x = −4 ± √13` · (b) `y = (x + 1)² − 9`, vertex (−1, −9).
> First: x squared plus eight x equals negative three; add sixteen: x plus four squared equals thirteen; x equals negative four plus or minus root thirteen. Second: y equals x plus one squared minus nine, with vertex negative one, negative nine.
> Remember: add half of b, squared, to both sides, and you can solve any quadratic, or find its vertex.

## 12.4 · The quadratic formula and the discriminant
- **Video:** `u12-l04-quadratic-formula`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 6:43 (speech 4:49 · pauses 89 s · 722 words · 8 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana knows where the quadratic formula comes from (completing the square on
`ax² + bx + c = 0`), reads a, b and c with their signs and solves any quadratic with it, and
uses the discriminant `b² − 4ac` to predict two, one or no real solutions, connect each case to
the graph, and decide whether the equation factors over the integers.

**Topics.**
- Completing the square once, for every equation; the formula.
- Reading a, b, c with signs; standard form first; exact and decimal answers.
- The discriminant: positive → two, zero → one, negative → none.
- Graph: crosses, touches, misses the x-axis.
- Perfect-square discriminant → rational roots, factorable.

#### S1 · Do it once, for all
*Screen:* `ax² + bx + c = 0`.
> Completing the square works on any quadratic, but it's a lot of steps. What if we did it once, with letters, and saved the result?
*Screen:* steps appear: `x² + (b/a)x = −c/a` → add `(b/2a)²` → `(x + b/2a)² = (b² − 4ac)/(4a²)` → `x + b/2a = ±√(b² − 4ac)/(2a)`.
> Divide by a, move c over a, add half of b over a, squared, to both sides. The left becomes a perfect square, x plus b over two a, squared. The right simplifies to b squared minus four a c, over four a squared. Take both roots.
*Screen:* `x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / (2a)`, framed.
> Subtract b over two a, and you get the quadratic formula: x equals negative b, plus or minus the square root of b squared minus four a c, all over two a.
> You don't have to memorize the derivation. But why is it worth seeing once?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the formula; countdown ring.
> Because then the formula isn't magic. It's just completing the square, done in advance.

#### S2 · Using it
*Screen:* `2x² + 3x − 5 = 0`: a = 2, b = 3, c = −5.
> Solve two x squared plus three x minus five equals zero. A is two, b is three, and c is negative five, with its sign.
*Screen:* `x = (−3 ± √(9 + 40))/4 = (−3 ± 7)/4` → `x = 1` or `x = −5/2`.
> B squared minus four a c is nine minus four times two times negative five: nine plus forty, forty-nine. Its root is seven. So x equals negative three plus or minus seven, over four. That's one, or negative five halves.

#### S3 · Standard form first
*Screen:* `x² = 4x + 1` → `x² − 4x − 1 = 0`: a = 1, b = −4, c = −1 → `x = (4 ± √20)/2 = 2 ± √5`.
> The equation must equal zero first. X squared equals four x plus one becomes x squared minus four x minus one equals zero. Now b is negative four, so negative b is positive four. The square root part is the root of sixteen plus four, root twenty, which is two root five. Divide everything by two: two plus or minus root five.
> Why do people often get the sign of negative b wrong? What's the safe habit?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `−b` with b = −4 → `−(−4) = 4`; countdown ring.
> When b is negative, negative b is positive. Writing b in parentheses, negative of the quantity negative four, keeps the signs straight.
*Screen:* decimals: `2 + √5 ≈ 4.24`, `2 − √5 ≈ −0.24`.
> As decimals, about four point two four and negative zero point two four.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* `3x² − 2x − 4 = 0`.
> Your turn. Solve three x squared minus two x minus four equals zero with the quadratic formula. Give exact answers. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `x = (2 ± √(4 + 48))/6 = (2 ± 2√13)/6 = (1 ± √13)/3`.
> A is three, b is negative two, c is negative four. B squared minus four a c is four plus forty-eight, fifty-two, which is two root thirteen. X equals two plus or minus two root thirteen, over six, which simplifies to one plus or minus root thirteen, over three.
> Remember: put the equation in standard form, read a, b, and c with their signs, and substitute carefully.

#### S5 · The part that decides
*Screen:* the formula; `b² − 4ac` highlighted.
> Look inside the quadratic formula at the part under the square root: b squared minus four a c. It's called the discriminant, because it discriminates, it tells cases apart.
> Thinking about square roots, what could happen if this number is positive? Zero? Negative?
[REFLECT 15s] *Screen:* three cards with `√(+)`, `√0`, `√(−)`; countdown ring.
*Screen:* positive → `± something` → two solutions · zero → `±0` → one solution · negative → no real root → no real solutions.
> If it's positive, its root is a real number, and plus or minus gives two different solutions. If it's zero, plus zero and minus zero are the same: one solution. If it's negative, there's no real square root: no real solutions.

#### S6 · What the graph shows
*Screen:* three parabolas: `y = x² − 4x + 3` (D = 4) crosses twice · `y = x² − 4x + 4` (D = 0) touches · `y = x² − 4x + 5` (D = −4) misses.
> Here are three parabolas that differ only in c. The first has discriminant four: it crosses the x axis twice. The second has discriminant zero: it touches the axis at its vertex. The third has discriminant negative four: it never reaches the axis.
> As c goes from three to five, what happens to the parabola, and why does the discriminant drop?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the parabola sliding up as c increases; countdown ring.
> Raising c lifts the parabola, and it pulls away from the x axis. In the discriminant, a bigger c makes four a c bigger, so b squared minus four a c gets smaller.

#### S7 · Does it factor?
*Screen:* `x² + 5x + 6` → D = 1 (perfect square) → factors · `x² + 5x + 3` → D = 13 → irrational roots, doesn't factor over integers.
> The discriminant also tells you if factoring is worth trying. If it's a perfect square, like one, four, or forty-nine, the solutions are rational, and the quadratic factors with integers. If it's positive but not a perfect square, the solutions are irrational, and you'll need the formula.

#### S8 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) `2x² + 4x + 5 = 0` (b) `9x² − 12x + 4 = 0` (c) `x² − 3x − 10 = 0`.
> Your turn. Use the discriminant to say how many real solutions each equation has, and whether it factors. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* (a) D = 16 − 40 = −24 → none · (b) D = 144 − 144 = 0 → one, `(3x − 2)²` · (c) D = 9 + 40 = 49 → two, factors `(x − 5)(x + 2)`.
> The first has discriminant negative twenty-four: no real solutions. The second has zero: one solution, and it's the perfect square three x minus two, squared. The third has forty-nine, a perfect square: two solutions, and it factors as x minus five times x plus two.
> Remember: the discriminant, b squared minus four a c, tells you how many real solutions there are before you solve.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 12.5 · Choosing the best method
- **Video:** `u12-l05-choosing-a-method`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 2:54 (speech 1:56 · pauses 44 s · 291 words · 4 scenes) · short

**Objective.** Ana chooses an efficient method (square roots, factoring, completing the
square, formula, graphing) by looking at the structure of the equation, and justifies the
choice.

**Topics.**
- No x term → square roots.
- Easy factors → factoring.
- a = 1, even b → completing the square.
- Anything else → formula; graphing to estimate or check.

#### S1 · Five tools
*Screen:* a toolbox with five labeled tools: square roots · factoring · completing the square · quadratic formula · graphing.
> You now have five ways to solve a quadratic equation. All of them work on the right equation, but some are much faster.
> Which tool would you reach for first, and why? Think about what each one needs.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the toolbox; countdown ring.

#### S2 · Read the structure
*Screen:* `5x² − 80 = 0` → "no x term → square roots" → `x² = 16`, `x = ±4`.
> If there's no x term, isolate x squared and take square roots. Five x squared minus eighty equals zero: x squared is sixteen, so x is plus or minus four.
*Screen:* `x² − x − 12 = 0` → D = 49 → "factors" → `(x − 4)(x + 3)`.
> If the numbers are small and it factors easily, factor. X squared minus x minus twelve is x minus four times x plus three.
*Screen:* `x² + 10x − 3 = 0` → "a = 1, b even → complete the square" → `(x + 5)² = 28`, `x = −5 ± 2√7`.
> If a is one and b is even, completing the square is quick. X squared plus ten x minus three: add twenty-five, and x plus five squared is twenty-eight.
*Screen:* `3.2x² − 1.7x − 4 = 0` → "messy → formula".
> If the numbers are messy, use the formula: it always works.
*Screen:* the graph of a quadratic with roots near −1 and 2.5.
> And graphing is great for estimating, or checking your answers.

#### S3 · Same equation, two ways
*Screen:* `x² − 6x + 5 = 0` by factoring (`(x − 1)(x − 5)`) and by the formula (`(6 ± 4)/2`).
> Solve x squared minus six x plus five both by factoring and with the formula. Do you get the same answers? Which was faster?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* both methods side by side; countdown ring.
> Both give one and five. Factoring took one line. The formula took several. The method doesn't change the answer, only the effort.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) `(x + 2)² = 7` (b) `x² + 4x = 0` (c) `2x² + 3x − 1 = 0` · choose a method and solve.
> Your turn. For each equation, pick the best method and solve it. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* (a) square roots: `x = −2 ± √7` · (b) factor: `x(x + 4) = 0` → `x = 0, −4` · (c) formula: `x = (−3 ± √17)/4`.
> The first is already a square: square roots give negative two plus or minus root seven. The second has a common factor: x times x plus four, so zero or negative four. The third doesn't factor, its discriminant is seventeen, so use the formula: negative three plus or minus root seventeen, over four.
> Remember: look at the equation before you start. Its structure tells you the fastest way.
> That's the end of solving quadratics. Next, the final unit: modeling.
