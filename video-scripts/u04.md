## 4.1 · Arithmetic sequences
- **Video:** `u04-l01-arithmetic-sequences`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:27 (speech 2:28 · pauses 42 s · 371 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana recognizes an arithmetic sequence by its common difference and writes it
both recursively and explicitly, using either formula to find terms.

**Topics.**
- Sequence, term, term number.
- Common difference d.
- Recursive formula: `a₁ = …, aₙ = aₙ₋₁ + d`.
- Explicit formula: `aₙ = a₁ + (n − 1)d`, and why it is `n − 1`.
- Which formula to use when.

#### S1 · Stacking chairs
*Screen:* a stack of chairs: 1 chair 80 cm, 2 chairs 92 cm, 3 chairs 104 cm, 4 chairs 116 cm.
> One chair is eighty centimeters tall. Each extra chair stacked on top adds twelve centimeters. Eighty, ninety-two, one hundred four, one hundred sixteen.
*Screen:* `80, 92, 104, 116, …` with term numbers 1, 2, 3, 4 above.
> A list of numbers in order is a sequence. Each number is a term. Here, the first term is eighty, the second ninety-two, and so on.
*Screen:* `+12` arrows between terms.
> When you add the same amount every time, it's an arithmetic sequence. The amount, twelve, is the common difference, d.

#### S2 · Recursive: from one to the next
*Screen:* `a₁ = 80`, `aₙ = aₙ₋₁ + 12`.
> One way to describe it says how to get from each term to the next. The first term is eighty. Each term is the one before it plus twelve. That's a recursive formula.
> What would you have to do to find the height of a stack of fifty chairs with this formula?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* a long chain of +12 arrows trailing off; countdown ring.
> You'd have to add twelve forty-nine times. It works, but it's slow.

#### S3 · Explicit: jump straight there
*Screen:* `a₅₀ = 80 + 12 + 12 + … + 12` (49 twelves) → `80 + 49 · 12`.
> Look at what you'd be adding. Fifty chairs means the first chair plus forty-nine more twelves.
> Why forty-nine, and not fifty?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* chairs numbered 1 to 5, arrows only between them (4 arrows); countdown ring.
> Because the first term doesn't get a twelve. Five chairs have only four steps between them. The number of steps is always one less than the term number.
*Screen:* `aₙ = a₁ + (n − 1)d` → `aₙ = 80 + 12(n − 1)`; `a₅₀ = 80 + 12 · 49 = 668`.
> So the explicit formula is a n equals a one plus the quantity n minus one, times d. For the chairs, eighty plus twelve times the quantity n minus one. Fifty chairs: eighty plus five hundred eighty-eight, six hundred sixty-eight centimeters.
*Screen:* simplified: `aₙ = 12n + 68`.
> Simplified, a n equals twelve n plus sixty-eight. It looks like a linear function, and it is one.

#### S4 · Going down
*Screen:* `20, 17, 14, 11, …`
> The difference can be negative. Twenty, seventeen, fourteen, eleven has a common difference of negative three.
*Screen:* recursive `a₁ = 20, aₙ = aₙ₋₁ − 3` · explicit `aₙ = 20 − 3(n − 1) = 23 − 3n`.
> Recursively: start at twenty, subtract three each time. Explicitly: a n equals twenty minus three times the quantity n minus one, or twenty-three minus three n.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* `−7, −2, 3, 8, …`: (a) recursive formula (b) explicit formula (c) the 30th term.
> Your turn. For negative seven, negative two, three, eight, write the recursive formula, the explicit formula, and find the thirtieth term. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `a₁ = −7, aₙ = aₙ₋₁ + 5` · `aₙ = −7 + 5(n − 1) = 5n − 12` · `a₃₀ = 138`.
> The difference is five. Recursively, start at negative seven and add five. Explicitly, a n equals five n minus twelve. The thirtieth term is one hundred fifty minus twelve, one hundred thirty-eight.
> Remember: arithmetic sequences add the same difference every time. Recursive formulas step, explicit formulas jump, and the jump uses n minus one steps.

## 4.2 · Geometric sequences
- **Video:** `u04-l02-geometric-sequences`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:30 (speech 2:26 · pauses 47 s · 365 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana recognizes a geometric sequence by its common ratio, writes recursive and
explicit formulas `aₙ = a₁ · rⁿ⁻¹`, and contrasts it with arithmetic growth.

**Topics.**
- Common ratio r: divide consecutive terms.
- Recursive: `aₙ = aₙ₋₁ · r`.
- Explicit: `aₙ = a₁ · rⁿ⁻¹`.
- Ratios between 0 and 1; negative ratios.
- Arithmetic vs. geometric.

#### S1 · A rumor
*Screen:* 1 person tells 3, each tells 3: tree diagram: 1, 3, 9, 27.
> One person tells a secret to three friends. Each of them tells three more, and so on. One, three, nine, twenty-seven.
> Is this an arithmetic sequence? Check the differences.
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* differences 2, 6, 18 appear; countdown ring.
*Screen:* `×3` arrows between terms.
> The differences are two, six, eighteen. Not constant. But every term is three times the one before. When you multiply by the same number each time, it's a geometric sequence. That number is the common ratio, r.

#### S2 · The formulas
*Screen:* `a₁ = 1, aₙ = aₙ₋₁ · 3`.
> Recursively: start at one, and multiply by three each time.
*Screen:* `a₅ = 1 · 3 · 3 · 3 · 3 = 1 · 3⁴`.
> To get to the fifth term, you multiply by three four times. Again, one less than the term number.
*Screen:* `aₙ = a₁ · rⁿ⁻¹` → `aₙ = 3ⁿ⁻¹`; `a₁₀ = 3⁹ = 19 683`.
> So the explicit formula is a n equals a one times r to the power n minus one. By the tenth round, nineteen thousand six hundred eighty-three people have heard it.
> Compare with arithmetic sequences. Where did the n minus one go this time, and why?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* `a₁ + (n − 1)d` above `a₁ · rⁿ⁻¹`; countdown ring.
> In arithmetic sequences, n minus one multiplies the difference, because we add it that many times. In geometric sequences, it's the exponent, because we multiply by r that many times.

#### S3 · Shrinking and flipping
*Screen:* a bouncing ball: 200, 100, 50, 25 cm; `r = 1/2`.
> A ratio between zero and one makes the terms shrink. A ball bounces to two hundred centimeters, then one hundred, then fifty. The ratio is one half.
*Screen:* `5, −10, 20, −40`; `r = −2`; the points alternate above and below zero.
> A negative ratio makes the signs alternate. Five, negative ten, twenty, negative forty has a ratio of negative two.
*Screen:* how to find r: `−10 ÷ 5 = −2`.
> To find r, divide any term by the one before it.

#### S4 · Growing faster
*Screen:* two columns: arithmetic `3, 6, 9, 12, …` (+3) and geometric `3, 6, 12, 24, …` (×2), both starting at 3, 6; a bar chart up to n = 10: 30 vs 1536.
> These two sequences both start three, six. One keeps adding three, the other keeps doubling. By the tenth term, the first is at thirty and the second at one thousand five hundred thirty-six.
> Will the arithmetic one ever catch up?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the bars; countdown ring.
> Never. Adding a fixed amount can't keep up with multiplying by a fixed factor. We'll see this again with exponential functions.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* `48, 12, 3, …`: (a) r (b) explicit formula (c) the 6th term.
> Your turn. For forty-eight, twelve, three, find the ratio, the explicit formula, and the sixth term. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `r = 1/4` · `aₙ = 48 · (1/4)ⁿ⁻¹` · `a₆ = 48/1024 = 3/64`.
> The ratio is one fourth. A n equals forty-eight times one fourth to the n minus one. The sixth term is forty-eight over one thousand twenty-four, which is three sixty-fourths.
> Remember: geometric sequences multiply by the same ratio. The explicit formula puts n minus one in the exponent.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 4.3 · Sequences are functions
- **Video:** `u04-l03-sequences-as-functions`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:01 (speech 2:06 · pauses 42 s · 314 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana sees a sequence as a function whose domain is the positive integers,
writes it with function notation, graphs it as separate points, and links arithmetic
sequences to linear functions and geometric sequences to exponential ones.

**Topics.**
- Term number as input, term as output.
- Domain: 1, 2, 3, …; discrete graphs (no connecting line).
- `f(n)` notation; recursive definitions in function notation.
- Arithmetic ↔ linear, geometric ↔ exponential.

#### S1 · Input and output
*Screen:* table n: 1, 2, 3, 4 · aₙ: 5, 8, 11, 14.
> Look at a sequence as a table. The term number n is the input. The term is the output. Each input gives exactly one output, so a sequence is a function.
*Screen:* `f(n) = 3n + 2`.
> We can write this one as f of n equals three n plus two. F of four is fourteen: the fourth term.

#### S2 · Dots, not a line
*Screen:* the points (1, 5), (2, 8), (3, 11), (4, 14) plotted; no line through them.
> When we graph it, we plot the points one, five; two, eight; and so on. But we don't connect them.
> Why not? What would a point at n equals two and a half mean?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* a ghost point at n = 2.5 with a question mark; countdown ring.
> There's no term number two and a half. The domain is only the positive integers: one, two, three, and so on. A graph made of separate points is called discrete.
*Screen:* the dots lie along the line `y = 3x + 2` drawn faintly.
> The dots do lie on a line, though: y equals three x plus two. An arithmetic sequence is a linear function, sampled only at whole numbers.

#### S3 · Recursive in function notation
*Screen:* `f(1) = 5, f(n) = f(n − 1) + 3 for n ≥ 2`.
> The recursive formula can also use function notation. F of one is five, and f of n equals f of n minus one, plus three.
*Screen:* geometric: `g(1) = 2, g(n) = 2 · g(n − 1)` → points 2, 4, 8, 16 curving up.
> A geometric sequence, like g of one equals two and g of n equals two times g of n minus one, makes dots that curve upward. They lie on an exponential curve.
> Here's a question to turn over. Could a sequence's graph go both up and down?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* an alternating sequence's dots; countdown ring.
> Yes. A geometric sequence with a negative ratio jumps above and below zero, like five, negative ten, twenty.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* a discrete graph: points (1, 40), (2, 34), (3, 28), (4, 22).
> Your turn. Write this sequence as a function, explicitly and recursively, and say whether it's arithmetic or geometric. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* arithmetic, d = −6 · `f(n) = 46 − 6n` · `f(1) = 40, f(n) = f(n − 1) − 6`.
> It drops six each time, so it's arithmetic. F of n equals forty-six minus six n. Recursively, f of one is forty, and each term is the previous one minus six.
> Remember: a sequence is a function on the positive integers. Arithmetic sequences are linear dots, geometric sequences are exponential dots.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 4.4 · Modeling with sequences
- **Video:** `u04-l04-modeling-with-sequences`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:22 (speech 2:25 · pauses 42 s · 362 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana decides whether a situation is arithmetic or geometric, builds the
sequence (choosing what term 1 means), and answers questions with it.

**Topics.**
- Add the same amount → arithmetic; multiply by the same factor → geometric.
- Choosing the starting term (n = 1 or n = 0).
- Answering "which term" questions.
- Limits of a model.

#### S1 · Two offers
*Screen:* Job A: $500 the first week, +$20 each week · Job B: $500 the first week, +3% each week.
> Two summer jobs. Job A pays five hundred dollars the first week, and twenty dollars more each week after. Job B pays five hundred the first week, and three percent more each week.
> Which job pays more in week ten? Make a guess before we compute.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* both offers; countdown ring.

#### S2 · Building the sequences
*Screen:* A: `aₙ = 500 + 20(n − 1)` → `a₁₀ = 680`.
> Job A adds the same amount each week, so it's arithmetic. The tenth week pays five hundred plus twenty times nine, six hundred eighty dollars.
*Screen:* B: `bₙ = 500 · 1.03ⁿ⁻¹` → `b₁₀ ≈ 652.39`.
> Job B grows by three percent, which means multiplying by one point zero three each week. That's geometric. The tenth week pays five hundred times one point zero three to the ninth, about six hundred fifty-two dollars.
> Why is growing by three percent the same as multiplying by one point zero three?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `500 + 0.03 · 500 = 500 · (1 + 0.03)`; countdown ring.
> Because you keep all of the old pay, which is one times it, and add three hundredths of it. One plus zero point zero three.

#### S3 · Which term?
*Screen:* a table of weeks 10 to 25 for both jobs; the B column passes A at week 21.
> In week ten, Job A pays more. But the geometric job keeps accelerating. Scanning a table, Job B pays more starting in week twenty-one.
*Screen:* summer = 12 weeks: A is better.
> If the job only lasts twelve weeks, Job A wins. The best model depends on the question.

#### S4 · Where to start counting
*Screen:* "A tree is 2 m tall when planted and grows 0.5 m a year." `h₀ = 2` vs `h₁ = 2`.
> Be careful with the first term. A tree is two meters tall when planted and grows half a meter a year. If n counts years after planting, it's natural to start at n equals zero: h of n equals two plus zero point five n.
> If you started at n equals one instead, the formula changes to two plus zero point five times the quantity n minus one. Say clearly what n means.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* "A video has 800 views on day 1. Views double every day." (a) model (b) on which day does it pass 100 000 views?
> Your turn. A video has eight hundred views on day one, and the views double every day. Write the model, and find the first day it passes one hundred thousand views. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `vₙ = 800 · 2ⁿ⁻¹`; day 8: 102 400.
> It's geometric with ratio two: v n equals eight hundred times two to the n minus one. Day seven has fifty-one thousand two hundred, day eight has one hundred two thousand four hundred. Day eight.
> Remember: decide whether the situation adds or multiplies, choose what the first term means, and check whether the model can really last.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.
