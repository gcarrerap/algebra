## 7.1 · Exponent rules
- **Video:** `u07-l01-exponent-rules`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:37 (speech 2:39 · pauses 42 s · 397 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana derives and applies the product, quotient and power rules (including power
of a product and of a quotient) by expanding repeated multiplication, and avoids the usual
mistakes (`x²·x³ ≠ x⁶`, `(x + y)² ≠ x² + y²`).

**Topics.**
- An exponent counts repeated factors.
- Product rule `aᵐ · aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ`.
- Quotient rule `aᵐ / aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ`.
- Power of a power `(aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ`; of a product `(ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ`; of a quotient.
- Traps: multiplying exponents in a product; powers of sums.

#### S1 · Counting factors
*Screen:* `2⁵ = 2 · 2 · 2 · 2 · 2 = 32`; base and exponent labeled.
> Two to the fifth means five twos multiplied together: thirty-two. The two is the base, the five is the exponent. An exponent counts how many times the base appears as a factor.
> Every rule in this lesson comes from just counting factors. If you forget a rule, you can always rebuild it.

#### S2 · Product and quotient
*Screen:* `x² · x³ = (x · x)(x · x · x) = x⁵`.
> X squared times x cubed is two x's times three x's. That's five x's: x to the fifth.
*Screen:* `aᵐ · aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ`.
> Multiplying powers of the same base, you add the exponents.
> A common mistake is to write x to the sixth. Why is that wrong?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `x⁶` crossed in red; countdown ring.
> Six would mean six factors of x, but there are only two plus three, five.
*Screen:* `x⁷ / x³ = (x·x·x·x·x·x·x)/(x·x·x)` → three pairs cancel → `x⁴`.
> Dividing, x to the seventh over x cubed: three x's on the bottom cancel three on top, leaving four. So you subtract the exponents: a to the m over a to the n is a to the m minus n.

#### S3 · Powers of powers and products
*Screen:* `(x³)² = x³ · x³ = x⁶`.
> X cubed, squared, means x cubed times x cubed. That's three plus three, six. A power of a power multiplies the exponents.
*Screen:* `(3x)² = 3x · 3x = 9x²`.
> Three x, squared, is three x times three x: nine x squared. The power reaches every factor inside: a b to the n is a to the n times b to the n.
*Screen:* `(2a³b)⁴ = 2⁴ a¹² b⁴ = 16a¹²b⁴`.
> So two a cubed b, to the fourth, is two to the fourth, times a to the twelfth, times b to the fourth: sixteen a to the twelfth b to the fourth.
*Screen:* `(x/y)³ = x³/y³`.
> And a quotient works the same: x over y, cubed, is x cubed over y cubed.

#### S4 · The sum trap
*Screen:* `(x + 3)²` = `x² + 9` ? with x = 1 test: `16` vs `10`.
> Now be careful. Is x plus three, squared, equal to x squared plus nine? Test it with x equal to one.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
> One plus three, squared, is sixteen. One plus nine is ten. They're not equal. Exponents spread over products, never over sums. We'll learn how to square a sum later.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* simplify `(4x²y)³ · x / (2xy²)`.
> Your turn. Simplify four x squared y, cubed, times x, over two x y squared. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `64x⁶y³ · x / (2xy²)` → `64x⁷y³ / (2xy²)` → `32x⁶y`.
> Four x squared y, cubed, is sixty-four x to the sixth y cubed. Times x gives x to the seventh. Dividing by two x y squared: sixty-four over two is thirty-two, seven minus one is six, three minus two is one. Thirty-two x to the sixth y.
> Remember: same base multiplied, add exponents. Divided, subtract. Power of a power, multiply. And when in doubt, write out the factors.

## 7.2 · Zero and negative exponents
- **Video:** `u07-l02-zero-and-negative-exponents`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:26 (speech 2:32 · pauses 40 s · 379 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana explains why `a⁰ = 1` and `a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ` from patterns and from the quotient
rule, and rewrites expressions with only positive exponents.

**Topics.**
- The halving pattern: 2³, 2², 2¹, 2⁰, 2⁻¹.
- Quotient rule: `a³/a³ = a⁰ = 1`.
- Negative exponents mean reciprocals.
- Simplifying with positive exponents only; `0⁰` is undefined.

#### S1 · Follow the pattern
*Screen:* a column: `2⁴ = 16`, `2³ = 8`, `2² = 4`, `2¹ = 2`, `2⁰ = ?`, with "÷ 2" arrows between rows.
> Look at the powers of two going down: sixteen, eight, four, two. Each time the exponent drops by one, the value is divided by two.
> So what must two to the zero be, to keep the pattern going?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the `?`; countdown ring.
*Screen:* `2⁰ = 1`.
> Two divided by two is one. Two to the zero is one.
*Screen:* the column continues: `2⁻¹ = 1/2`, `2⁻² = 1/4`, `2⁻³ = 1/8`.
> Keep going. Two to the negative one is one half. Two to the negative two is one fourth. Negative exponents give fractions.

#### S2 · The quotient rule agrees
*Screen:* `x⁵/x⁵ = 1` and `x⁵/x⁵ = x⁵⁻⁵ = x⁰` → `x⁰ = 1`.
> The quotient rule tells the same story. Anything divided by itself is one. But the rule says x to the fifth over x to the fifth is x to the zero. Both must be right, so x to the zero equals one.
*Screen:* `x²/x⁵ = (x·x)/(x·x·x·x·x) = 1/x³` and `= x⁻³`.
> And x squared over x to the fifth: cancel two x's, and three are left on the bottom, one over x cubed. The rule gives x to the negative three. So x to the negative three means one over x cubed.
*Screen:* `a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ` (a ≠ 0).
> A negative exponent means the reciprocal: a to the negative n is one over a to the n.
> Is two to the negative three a negative number?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `2⁻³ = −8 ?` with a question mark; countdown ring.
> No. It's one eighth, a small positive number. The negative exponent says "flip", not "make negative".

#### S3 · Simplifying
*Screen:* `3x⁻²` → `3/x²` (only x moves) vs. `(3x)⁻² = 1/(9x²)`.
> Watch what the exponent is attached to. In three x to the negative two, only the x has the exponent, so it's three over x squared. But in three x, all to the negative two, it's one over nine x squared.
*Screen:* `a⁻³b² / c⁻⁴ = b²c⁴ / a³`.
> A factor with a negative exponent can move across the fraction bar and its exponent becomes positive. A to the negative three, b squared, over c to the negative four, becomes b squared c to the fourth over a cubed.
*Screen:* `0⁰` with a note "undefined".
> One exception: zero to the zero is undefined, because zero over zero has no value.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* simplify with positive exponents: `(2x⁻³y²)⁻²`.
> Your turn. Simplify two x to the negative three, y squared, all to the negative two, using only positive exponents. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `2⁻² x⁶ y⁻⁴` → `x⁶ / (4y⁴)`.
> The power of a power gives two to the negative two, x to the sixth, y to the negative four. Then move the negative exponents down: x to the sixth over four y to the fourth.
> Remember: any nonzero number to the zero is one, and a negative exponent means take the reciprocal.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 7.3 · Roots and simplifying radicals
- **Video:** `u07-l03-roots-and-radicals`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:23 (speech 2:26 · pauses 42 s · 365 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana finds square and cube roots, knows the principal square root is
nonnegative, simplifies radicals with perfect-square factors, and distinguishes rational
from irrational roots.

**Topics.**
- Square root as the inverse of squaring; principal root.
- Cube roots, including of negative numbers.
- Perfect squares; irrational roots between integers.
- `√(ab) = √a · √b`; simplifying `√72`.
- Why `√(a + b) ≠ √a + √b`.

#### S1 · Undoing a square
*Screen:* a square of area 49 → side 7; `√49 = 7`.
> A square has an area of forty-nine. How long is its side? Seven, because seven squared is forty-nine. The square root undoes squaring.
*Screen:* `7² = 49`, `(−7)² = 49`; `√49 = 7` only.
> Negative seven squared is also forty-nine. But the square root symbol means the principal root, the nonnegative one. Square root of forty-nine is seven.
*Screen:* `∛8 = 2`, `∛(−27) = −3`.
> A cube root undoes cubing. The cube root of eight is two, and the cube root of negative twenty-seven is negative three, because negative three cubed is negative twenty-seven.
> Why can a cube root of a negative number exist, when a square root of a negative number can't be a real number?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* `√(−9) = ?` and `∛(−8) = −2`; countdown ring.
> Squaring any real number, positive or negative, gives something nonnegative, so nothing squares to negative nine. Cubing keeps the sign, so negative numbers have cube roots.

#### S2 · Between the squares
*Screen:* perfect squares 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64…; `√20` placed between 4 and 5 on a number line.
> Perfect squares, like one, four, nine, sixteen, have whole-number roots. Others don't. Twenty is between sixteen and twenty-five, so its root is between four and five, about four point four seven.
> Roots like this are irrational: their decimals never end and never repeat.

#### S3 · Simplifying
*Screen:* `√(4 · 9) = √36 = 6` and `√4 · √9 = 2 · 3 = 6`.
> Square roots split over products. The root of four times nine is six, and so is the root of four times the root of nine.
*Screen:* `√72 = √(36 · 2) = 6√2`.
> That lets us simplify. Seventy-two is thirty-six times two, and thirty-six is a perfect square. So the root of seventy-two is six times the root of two.
> What if you'd used nine times eight instead of thirty-six times two?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `√72 = √9 · √8 = 3√8`; countdown ring.
*Screen:* `3√8 = 3√(4·2) = 6√2`.
> You'd get three root eight, which isn't finished, because eight still has the square factor four. Keep going, and you reach six root two. Using the largest square factor gets there in one step.
*Screen:* `√(9 + 16) = 5` but `√9 + √16 = 7`.
> But square roots do not split over sums. The root of nine plus sixteen is five, not seven.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* simplify (a) `√50` (b) `√(48x²)` for x ≥ 0 (c) `2√27`.
> Your turn. Simplify the root of fifty, the root of forty-eight x squared, and two times the root of twenty-seven. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `5√2` · `4x√3` · `6√3`.
> The root of fifty is five root two. The root of forty-eight x squared is four x root three. Two root twenty-seven is two times three root three, six root three.
> Remember: roots undo powers, the square root is never negative, and you simplify by pulling out perfect-square factors.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 7.4 · Rational exponents
- **Video:** `u07-l04-rational-exponents`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:20 (speech 2:22 · pauses 44 s · 356 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana explains why `a^(1/n)` is the n-th root, rewrites `a^(m/n)` as
`(ⁿ√a)ᵐ`, evaluates and simplifies expressions with rational exponents using the exponent
rules.

**Topics.**
- What should `9^(1/2)` mean? Using the power rule.
- `a^(1/n) = ⁿ√a`.
- `a^(m/n) = (ⁿ√a)ᵐ = ⁿ√(aᵐ)`; root first is easier.
- Negative rational exponents.
- Converting between radical and exponent form.

#### S1 · A half power?
*Screen:* `9^(1/2) = ?`
> What could nine to the one half possibly mean? You can't multiply nine by itself half a time.
> Use the power of a power rule. What is nine to the one half, squared?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* `(9^(1/2))² = 9^(½ · 2) = 9¹`; countdown ring.
> One half times two is one, so it's nine. Nine to the one half is a number whose square is nine. That's the square root of nine: three.
*Screen:* `a^(1/2) = √a`, `a^(1/3) = ∛a`, `a^(1/n) = ⁿ√a`.
> In general, a to the one over n is the n-th root of a. Eight to the one third is the cube root of eight, two.

#### S2 · Numerator and denominator
*Screen:* `8^(2/3) = (8^(1/3))² = 2² = 4`.
> Now eight to the two thirds. Split the exponent: two thirds is one third times two. Take the cube root of eight, which is two, then square it: four.
*Screen:* `a^(m/n) = (ⁿ√a)ᵐ = ⁿ√(aᵐ)`; "denominator = root, numerator = power".
> The denominator is the root, the numerator is the power. You can do them in either order.
> Which order is easier for eighty-one to the three fourths? Try both in your head.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* `(⁴√81)³` vs. `⁴√(81³)`; countdown ring.
*Screen:* `⁴√81 = 3`, `3³ = 27`.
> Root first: the fourth root of eighty-one is three, and three cubed is twenty-seven. Power first would mean finding the fourth root of five hundred thirty-one thousand four hundred forty-one. Root first keeps the numbers small.

#### S3 · Negative and mixed
*Screen:* `32^(−3/5) = 1 / 32^(3/5) = 1 / (⁵√32)³ = 1/2³ = 1/8`.
> A negative rational exponent still means reciprocal. Thirty-two to the negative three fifths is one over thirty-two to the three fifths. The fifth root of thirty-two is two, cubed is eight. So it's one eighth.
*Screen:* `x^(1/2) · x^(1/3) = x^(5/6) = ⁶√(x⁵)`.
> All the exponent rules still work. X to the one half times x to the one third is x to the five sixths, which is the sixth root of x to the fifth.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) `27^(2/3)` (b) `16^(−1/4)` (c) write `⁵√(x³)` with an exponent.
> Your turn. Evaluate twenty-seven to the two thirds and sixteen to the negative one fourth, and write the fifth root of x cubed with an exponent. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `9` · `1/2` · `x^(3/5)`.
> The cube root of twenty-seven is three, squared is nine. Sixteen to the one fourth is two, so the answer is one half. And the fifth root of x cubed is x to the three fifths.
> Remember: a fractional exponent is a root and a power at once. The denominator is the root, the numerator is the power.
> That completes exponents and radicals. Next, exponential functions.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.
