## 0.1a · Order of operations
- **Video:** `u00-l01-order-of-operations`
- **Status:** produced
- **Estimated length:** 3:21 (speech 2:08 · pauses 50 s · 320 words · 6 scenes)

*Produced video (v1) differs from this script: its pauses are 1.5–4 s, and the lines marked
(new) were not recorded. Applying this script needs only those blocks' audio; pauses are silence.*

**Objective.** Ana evaluates any numeric expression in the agreed order (groups, exponents,
multiplication and division left to right, addition and subtraction left to right), and knows
why `−3²` and `(−3)²` are different numbers.

**Topics.**
- Why an agreed order is needed (two people, two answers).
- PEMDAS as four levels, not six steps.
- Same level → left to right (`12 ÷ 3 × 2`).
- A full example with every level.
- The negative sign and the exponent: `−3²` vs `(−3)²`.

#### S1 · The puzzle
*Screen:* `2 + 3 × 4`, large, centered.
> Here's a quick one. What is two plus three times four?
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* the expression stays; countdown ring.
*Screen:* wrong path in red: `(2 + 3) × 4 = 20`.
> If you said twenty, you're not alone. Two plus three is five, and five times four is twenty.
*Screen:* `2 + 3 × 4 = 14`, the `3 × 4` in yellow (MD).
> But the correct answer is fourteen.
> Math has an agreed order for doing operations, so that everyone gets the same answer.
> Think about it for a moment. What would happen if every calculator, and every person, used a different order?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* two calculators showing 20 and 14 side by side. *(new)*

#### S2 · The order
*Screen:* PEMDAS letters appear one by one, each in its color: P blue · E purple · MD yellow · AS green.
> The order is often remembered as PEMDAS.
> First, parentheses, and anything else that groups numbers together.
*Screen:* under P: `( )  [ ]  fraction bar  | |`.
> Second, exponents.
> Third, multiplication and division, together, from left to right.
*Screen:* M and D share one yellow box.
> Last, addition and subtraction, together, also from left to right.
*Screen:* A and S share one green box.

#### S3 · Left to right
*Screen:* `12 ÷ 3 × 2`.
> That left to right part is where most mistakes happen. Try twelve divided by three, times two.
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `12 ÷ 3` highlighted yellow → `4 × 2`.
> Multiplication does not go first. Division comes first here, because it is on the left. Twelve divided by three is four.
*Screen:* `8`.
> Then four times two is eight.

#### S4 · A full example
*Screen:* `3 + 2 × (8 − 5)²`.
> Now everything at once. Three plus two times the quantity eight minus five, squared.
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* "What goes first?" under the expression.
*Screen:* each step appears on the word that says its result, in its level's color.
> Parentheses first. Eight minus five is three.
> Then the exponent. Three squared is nine.
> Then multiply. Two times nine is eighteen.
> And finally add. Three plus eighteen is twenty-one.

#### S5 · Watch the negative sign
*Screen:* `−3²   vs   (−3)²`.
> One more trap, with negative numbers. Look at these two.
> Before I tell you, decide for yourself: are they the same number?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* both expressions, a question mark between them. *(new)*
*Screen:* `(−3)² = (−3)(−3) = 9`.
> In the quantity negative three, squared, the parentheses say the negative sign belongs to the three. Negative three times negative three is positive nine.
*Screen:* `−3² = −(3 · 3) = −9`.
> But without parentheses, the exponent goes first. Three squared is nine, and then the negative sign makes it negative nine.

#### S6 · Your turn
*Screen:* `10 − 4 + 2 × 3`.

[TRY IT 15s] *Screen:* the problem stays; countdown ring.
*Screen:* `10 − 4 + 6 = 12`.
> Multiply first. Two times three is six. Then, left to right, ten minus four is six, and six plus six is twelve.
*Screen:* recap strip `P · E · MD · AS`.
> Remember: groups, exponents, multiply and divide, then add and subtract. See you in the next lesson.

## 0.1b · Signed numbers
- **Video:** `u00-l01-signed-numbers`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 4:28 (speech 3:15 · pauses 50 s · 487 words · 6 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana adds, subtracts, multiplies and divides positive and negative numbers
with confidence, reads absolute value as distance from zero, and can explain each sign rule
with the number line instead of memorizing it.

**Topics.**
- The number line; opposites; absolute value as distance.
- Adding signed numbers as moves on the line.
- Subtracting = adding the opposite (`5 − (−2) = 5 + 2`).
- Multiplying and dividing: the sign rules and why negative times negative is positive.
- Evaluating an expression at a negative value (substitute in parentheses).
- Signed numbers inside the order of operations.

#### S1 · Below zero
*Screen:* a thermometer at 3 degrees; it drops to −5.
> It's three degrees outside, and the temperature drops eight degrees. What's the temperature now?
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* horizontal number line from −10 to 10; an arrow from 3 moves 8 to the left, lands on −5.
> Negative five. Numbers below zero are negative numbers, and the number line holds all of them.
> Every number has an opposite, the same distance from zero on the other side. The opposite of five is negative five.
*Screen:* 5 and −5 glow; equal brackets show the distance 5.
> That distance from zero is called the absolute value. The absolute value of negative five is five, and so is the absolute value of five.
*Screen:* `|−5| = 5`, `|5| = 5`.
> Here's something to think about. Can an absolute value ever be negative?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `|?| = −3 ?`
> No. A distance can't be negative. The smallest absolute value is zero.

#### S2 · Adding is moving
*Screen:* number line; `−4 + 6`.
> Adding is moving along the line. Adding a positive number moves right. Adding a negative number moves left.
*Screen:* start at −4, arrow 6 to the right, land on 2.
> Negative four plus six: start at negative four and move six to the right. You land on two.
*Screen:* `−3 + (−5)`, arrow from −3 five to the left, land on −8.
> Negative three plus negative five: start at negative three and move five more to the left. Negative eight.
*Screen:* summary in two cards: "same signs → add the distances, keep the sign" · "different signs → subtract the distances, keep the sign of the longer one".
> So with the same signs, add the distances and keep the sign. With different signs, subtract the distances and keep the sign of the number farther from zero.

#### S3 · Subtracting is adding the opposite
*Screen:* `5 − 2` and `5 + (−2)` side by side; both arrows land on 3.
> Five minus two is three. Five plus negative two is also three. Subtracting a number is the same as adding its opposite.
*Screen:* `5 − (−2)` appears.
> Now the strange one. Five minus negative two. Pause and decide which way to move.
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the number line, a question mark over 5.
*Screen:* `5 − (−2) = 5 + 2 = 7`; the two minus signs turn green and merge into a plus.
> Subtracting negative two means adding its opposite, positive two. Five plus two is seven. Taking away a debt leaves you richer.

#### S4 · Multiplying and dividing
*Screen:* `3 × (−4)` as three jumps of −4 on the line, landing on −12.
> Three times negative four is three jumps of negative four. That's negative twelve.
*Screen:* a pattern table: `3 × (−4) = −12`, `2 × (−4) = −8`, `1 × (−4) = −4`, `0 × (−4) = 0`, `−1 × (−4) = ?`
> Now watch the pattern as the first number goes down by one. Each answer goes up by four.
> What has to come next, if the pattern keeps going?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the next rows wait with question marks.
*Screen:* `−1 × (−4) = 4`, `−2 × (−4) = 8`.
> Four, then eight. A negative times a negative is positive. It isn't a rule someone invented; the pattern forces it.
*Screen:* sign table: `+·+ = +`, `−·− = +`, `+·− = −`, `−·+ = −`; "same for division".
> So same signs give a positive answer, different signs give a negative one. Division follows the same rules, because division undoes multiplication. Negative twelve divided by negative three is positive four.

#### S5 · Plugging in a negative
*Screen:* `x² + 2x − 1` and "x = −3".
> Signs matter most when you put a negative number into an expression. Evaluate x squared plus two x minus one, when x equals negative three.
*Screen:* `(−3)² + 2(−3) − 1`; the parentheses glow blue.
> Replace every x with negative three, inside parentheses. That keeps the sign attached to the number.
*Screen:* `9 − 6 − 1 = 2`; next to it in red: `−3² = −9` ✗.
> Negative three, squared, is nine. Two times negative three is negative six. Nine minus six minus one is two. Without the parentheses, you'd square only the three and get the sign wrong.

#### S6 · Your turn
*Screen:* `−5 − (−2) × 4`.
> Your turn. Negative five minus negative two times four. Use the order of operations, and watch every sign. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 15s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `−5 − (−8)` → `−5 + 8` → `3`; MD step yellow, AS step green.
> Multiply first. Negative two times four is negative eight. Then negative five minus negative eight is negative five plus eight, which is three.
*Screen:* recap cards.
> Remember: adding moves along the line, subtracting adds the opposite, and for multiplying and dividing, same signs are positive and different signs are negative.
> Next, the properties of operations.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 0.2a · Commutative and associative properties
- **Video:** `u00-l02-commutative-associative`
- **Status:** produced
- **Estimated length:** 4:18 (speech 3:10 · pauses 40 s · 475 words · 7 scenes)

*Produced video (v1) differs from this script: its pauses are 1.8–4 s, and the lines marked
(new) were not recorded.*

**Objective.** Ana recognizes the commutative property (order) and the associative property
(grouping), knows they hold for addition and multiplication but not for subtraction and
division, and uses them to compute mentally.

**Topics.**
- A mental-math shortcut that needs a reason (`25 × 7 × 4`).
- Commutative: `a + b = b + a`, `ab = ba`.
- Counterexamples with subtraction and division.
- Associative: `(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)`.
- Explaining the shortcut with both properties.

#### S1 · The shortcut
*Screen:* `25 × 7 × 4`.
> Here's a quick one. What is twenty-five times seven times four?
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* slow path `25 × 7 = 175, 175 × 4 = ?` dims.
> Most people start with twenty-five times seven, which is one hundred seventy-five. Then they multiply that by four. That's a lot of work.
*Screen:* fast path `25 × 4 = 100, 100 × 7 = 700`.
> Here's a shortcut. Twenty-five times four is one hundred, and one hundred times seven is seven hundred.
> But why are we allowed to move numbers around like that? Two properties say we can.
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the two paths side by side, "Why is this allowed?" *(pause new)*

#### S2 · Commutative: order
*Screen:* title card "Commutative" in blue.
> The first one is the commutative property. It's about order.
*Screen:* `3 + 5 = 5 + 3`.
> Three plus five is eight. Five plus three is also eight.
*Screen:* `4 × 6 = 6 × 4`.
> Swapping the order doesn't change the sum. Multiplication works the same way. Four times six and six times four are both twenty-four.
*Screen:* `a + b = b + a,  a · b = b · a`.
> With letters, a plus b equals b plus a, and a times b equals b times a.
> Think of the word commute. The numbers move around and trade places.

#### S3 · Not for subtraction
*Screen:* `8 − 3 = 5,  3 − 8 = −5`.
> But careful. Does it work for subtraction? Eight minus three is five. Three minus eight is negative five.
*Screen:* `12 ÷ 4 = 3,  4 ÷ 12 = 1/3`.
> Not the same. And twelve divided by four is three, but four divided by twelve is only one third.
> Subtraction and division are not commutative. With them, order matters.

#### S4 · Associative: grouping
*Screen:* title card "Associative" in purple.
> The second one is the associative property. It's about grouping.
*Screen:* `(2 + 3) + 4 = 5 + 4 = 9`.
> Take two plus three, plus four. Group the first two. Two plus three is five, and five plus four is nine.
*Screen:* `2 + (3 + 4) = 2 + 7 = 9`.
> Now group the last two instead. Three plus four is seven, and two plus seven is nine.
> Same answer. The numbers stay in the same order. Only the parentheses move.
*Screen:* `(a + b) + c = a + (b + c),  (a · b) · c = a · (b · c)`.
> With letters, the quantity a plus b, plus c, equals a plus the quantity b plus c. It works for multiplication too.
> Think of the word associate, like the friends you hang out with. Each number keeps its place in line, but it changes who it's grouped with.

#### S5 · Not for subtraction either
*Screen:* `(10 − 4) − 3 = 3`.
> Subtraction breaks this rule too. Ten minus four, minus three, is six minus three, which is three.
*Screen:* `10 − (4 − 3) = 9`.
> But ten minus the quantity four minus three is ten minus one, which is nine.
> Why do you think subtraction breaks both rules? Think about what the minus sign is attached to.
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* both results, 3 and 9, in red. *(new)*

#### S6 · The shortcut, explained
*Screen:* `25 × 7 × 4` → `25 × 4 × 7` (blue swap arrow) → `(25 × 4) × 7` (purple parentheses) → `100 × 7 = 700`.
> Now we can explain the shortcut. Start with twenty-five times seven times four.
> The commutative property lets us swap the seven and the four.
> The associative property lets us group the twenty-five with the four.
> Twenty-five times four is one hundred, and one hundred times seven is seven hundred.
> The trick is to look for pairs that make tens or hundreds.

#### S7 · Your turn
*Screen:* `38 + 17 + 62`.
> Your turn. What is thirty-eight plus seventeen plus sixty-two? Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 15s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `(38 + 62) + 17 = 100 + 17 = 117`.
> Swap and group so that thirty-eight and sixty-two go together. That makes one hundred. Then one hundred plus seventeen is one hundred seventeen.
> Remember: commutative changes the order, and associative changes the grouping. Both work for adding and multiplying, but not for subtracting or dividing.
> Next, the distributive property.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 0.2b · Distributive property
- **Video:** `u00-l02-distributive`
- **Status:** produced
- **Estimated length:** 3:30 (speech 2:24 · pauses 45 s · 361 words · 5 scenes)

*Produced video (v1) differs from this script: its pauses are 1.5–4 s, and the lines marked
(new) were not recorded.*

**Objective.** Ana expands `a(b + c)` into `ab + ac`, explains it with the area of a
rectangle, and carries the sign of the outside factor to every term.

**Topics.**
- Mental math: `6 × 98 = 6 × (100 − 2)`.
- Area model: one rectangle, two ways to measure it.
- With a variable: `3(x + 4) = 3x + 12`.
- Negative factors and the "only the first term" mistake.

#### S1 · Mental math
*Screen:* `6 × 98`.
> Try this one in your head. What is six times ninety-eight?
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `6 × (100 − 2)` → `600 − 12` → `588`.
> Here's a trick. Ninety-eight is one hundred minus two.
> Six times one hundred is six hundred. Six times two is twelve.
> And six hundred minus twelve is five hundred eighty-eight. You just used the distributive property.

#### S2 · Why it works
*Screen:* a 3-by-(4 + 2) rectangle; the width split into 4 and 2.
> Here's why it works. Picture a rectangle three units tall. Its width is four plus two.
*Screen:* whole rectangle shaded: `3 × 6 = 18`.
> One way to find the area is to add first. Four plus two is six, and three times six is eighteen.
*Screen:* two rectangles, `3 × 4` and `3 × 2`: `12 + 6 = 18`.
> Another way is to split it into two rectangles. Three times four is twelve, and three times two is six. Twelve plus six is eighteen.
> Same rectangle, same area. Multiplying a sum gives the same result as multiplying each part and adding.
> Look at the rectangle again, and ask yourself: would this still work if the width were split into three pieces?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the rectangle with a third dashed piece. *(new)*

#### S3 · With a variable
*Screen:* `3(x + 4)`; rectangle with width `x` and `4`.
> This is what makes it so useful in algebra. What is three times the quantity x plus four?
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
> We can't add x and four, because we don't know what x is. But we can still split the rectangle.
*Screen:* parts `3x` and `12`; arrows from 3 to each term.
> Three times x is three x. Three times four is twelve.
> So three times the quantity x plus four equals three x plus twelve. The number outside multiplies every term inside.
*Screen:* `a(b + c) = ab + ac`.
> With letters, a times the quantity b plus c equals a b plus a c.

#### S4 · Watch the signs
*Screen:* `−2(x − 5)`.
> Now watch the signs. What is negative two times the quantity x minus five?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* countdown ring; arrows from −2 wait.
*Screen:* `−2x`, then `−2x + 10`.
> Negative two times x is negative two x.
> Negative two times negative five is positive ten. So the answer is negative two x plus ten.
*Screen:* `−2x − 5` crossed in red.
> A very common mistake is to multiply only the first term, and write negative two x minus five. The arrows must reach every term, and the sign goes along.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* `4(2x + 3)`.
> Your turn. Expand four times the quantity two x plus three. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 15s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `8x + 12`.
> Four times two x is eight x. Four times three is twelve. The answer is eight x plus twelve.
> Remember: the number outside is shared with every term inside, and its sign goes along.
> Next, the parts of an expression.

## 0.3 · Parts of an expression
- **Video:** `u00-l03-parts-of-an-expression`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:28 (speech 2:30 · pauses 40 s · 374 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana names the terms, factors, coefficients and constants of an expression,
including hidden coefficients (`x` means `1x`, `−x` means `−1x`) and signs that belong to the
term after them.

**Topics.**
- Expression vs. equation.
- Terms: the pieces separated by plus and minus; the sign travels with the term.
- Factors: what is multiplied inside a term.
- Coefficient and constant; coefficients of one and negative one.
- A quantity in parentheses counts as one factor.

#### S1 · A recipe made of pieces
*Screen:* `4x² − x + 7`, large.
> This is an algebraic expression. It has numbers, a variable, and operations, but no equals sign.
*Screen:* an equation `4x² − x + 7 = 10` appears below and is labeled "equation".
> With an equals sign, it would be an equation, a statement that two things are equal. An expression is just a quantity. It doesn't claim anything.
> Knowing the names of its parts lets us talk about it clearly. Let's take it apart.

#### S2 · Terms
*Screen:* the expression; three boxes slide around `4x²`, `− x`, `+ 7`.
> Terms are the pieces that are added or subtracted. This expression has three terms: four x squared, negative x, and seven.
*Screen:* the minus sign glows and joins the box of `x`.
> Notice the minus sign. It belongs to the term after it. The second term is not x. It is negative x.
*Screen:* `3a + 2b − 5c + 1`.
> How many terms does this expression have, and what is the third one?
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* four boxes; the third, `−5c`, glows.
> Four terms. The third term is negative five c, with its sign.

#### S3 · Factors and coefficients
*Screen:* zoom into `4x²` = `4 · x · x`.
> Inside a term, the things being multiplied are factors. Four x squared is four times x times x.
*Screen:* the `4` turns yellow, label "coefficient".
> The number factor in front of a variable is the coefficient. The coefficient of four x squared is four.
*Screen:* `−x` = `−1 · x`.
> Now, what is the coefficient of negative x? There's no number written there.
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `−x`, a question mark above it.
*Screen:* a ghostly `1` appears: `−1x`.
> The coefficient is negative one. A variable with no number in front has a coefficient of one, and a minus sign makes it negative one.
*Screen:* `+7` glows green, label "constant".
> A term with no variable is a constant. Seven is the constant term. Its value never changes, whatever x is.

#### S4 · Groups count as one factor
*Screen:* `5(x + 2) − 3y`.
> One more case. In five times the quantity x plus two, minus three y, how many terms are there?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* two boxes: `5(x + 2)` and `− 3y`.
> Two. The parentheses act as one group, so five times the quantity x plus two is a single term with two factors: five, and the quantity x plus two.
> Inside the parentheses there are two more terms, x and two. Parts can contain parts.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* `−2m³ + m − 9` and a table: terms · coefficients · constant.
> Your turn. For negative two m cubed plus m minus nine, list the terms, the coefficient of each variable term, and the constant. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 15s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* the table fills in: `−2m³, m, −9` · `−2, 1` · `−9`.
> Three terms: negative two m cubed, m, and negative nine. The coefficients are negative two and one. The constant is negative nine, sign included.
> Remember: terms are separated by plus and minus and keep their sign, factors are multiplied, and every variable term has a coefficient, even when you can't see it.

## 0.4 · Combining like terms
- **Video:** `u00-l04-like-terms`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:36 (speech 2:44 · pauses 35 s · 409 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana recognizes like terms (same variables with the same exponents), combines
them by adding coefficients, and simplifies expressions that need the distributive property
first.

**Topics.**
- Like terms: same variable part; numbers don't matter.
- Why combining works: it is the distributive property backwards.
- Unlike terms that look alike (`x` and `x²`).
- Simplifying: distribute, then collect, keeping signs.

#### S1 · Apples and apples
*Screen:* 3 apples + 2 apples = 5 apples; then 3 apples + 2 bananas.
> Three apples plus two apples is five apples. But three apples plus two bananas is not five of anything. It just stays three apples plus two bananas.
*Screen:* `3x + 2x = 5x`; `3x + 2y` stays.
> Algebra works the same way. Three x plus two x is five x. But three x plus two y can't be combined.
> Terms that can be combined are called like terms. They have exactly the same variable part.

#### S2 · What makes terms alike
*Screen:* pairs to sort into "like" and "not like": `4a, −7a` · `x, x²` · `5xy, 2yx` · `6, −1`.
> Let's sort some pairs. Four a and negative seven a are like terms. Six and negative one are like terms too: they are both constants.
> Now x and x squared. They both have x. Are they like terms?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `x` and `x²` with a question mark; countdown ring.
*Screen:* `x²` drawn as a square, `x` as a line segment.
> No. The exponents are different. If x is a length, x squared is an area. You can't add lengths and areas into one number of anything.
*Screen:* `5xy` and `2yx` move into "like".
> And five x y and two y x are like terms, because multiplication is commutative: y x is the same as x y.

#### S3 · Why adding coefficients works
*Screen:* `3x + 2x = (3 + 2)x = 5x`, the `x` pulled out in yellow.
> Why can we just add the coefficients? It's the distributive property, read backwards. Three x plus two x equals the quantity three plus two, times x. That's five x.
*Screen:* `7y − 9y = −2y`.
> It works with subtraction too. Seven y minus nine y is negative two y.

#### S4 · Simplifying step by step
*Screen:* `4(x − 2) − 3x + 10`.
> Now a full simplification. Four times the quantity x minus two, minus three x, plus ten.
> Before you start, what must happen first, and why?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* countdown ring; the parentheses glow blue.
*Screen:* `4x − 8 − 3x + 10` (DIST yellow).
> First distribute. Four times x is four x, and four times negative two is negative eight.
*Screen:* like terms tinted: x-terms blue, constants green; then `x + 2`.
> Now collect like terms, each with its sign. Four x minus three x is x. Negative eight plus ten is two. The simplified expression is x plus two.
*Screen:* check panel: x = 5 → `4(3) − 15 + 10 = 7` and `5 + 2 = 7`.
> You can check by picking a number for x. If x is five, the original is seven, and x plus two is also seven.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* `2(3a + 1) − (a − 4)`.
> Your turn. Simplify two times the quantity three a plus one, minus the quantity a minus four. Careful with the minus sign in front of the second parentheses. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 15s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `6a + 2 − a + 4` → `5a + 6`; the "−(a − 4)" step shows `−1(a − 4)`.
> Two times the quantity three a plus one is six a plus two. The minus in front means negative one times the quantity a minus four, which is negative a plus four. Six a minus a is five a, and two plus four is six. The answer is five a plus six.
> Remember: like terms have the same variables with the same exponents, you combine them by adding their coefficients, and you distribute before you combine.
> Next, translating words into expressions.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 0.5 · Translating words into expressions
- **Video:** `u00-l05-words-to-expressions`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:15 (speech 2:19 · pauses 40 s · 347 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana translates verbal descriptions into algebraic expressions, choosing the
variable, the operation, and the order correctly, especially for "less than", "subtracted
from" and "the quantity".

**Topics.**
- Choosing and defining a variable.
- Key words for each operation.
- Order traps: "five less than x" is `x − 5`.
- Grouping: "twice the sum of" vs. "twice a number plus".
- From a real situation to an expression.

#### S1 · A phone plan
*Screen:* a phone bill card: "$25 a month plus $4 for each gigabyte".
> A phone plan costs twenty-five dollars a month, plus four dollars for each gigabyte of data. How could we write the cost for any month?
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
