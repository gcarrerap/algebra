## 10.1 · Greatest common factor
- **Video:** `u10-l01-greatest-common-factor`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:52 (speech 2:54 · pauses 42 s · 435 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana understands factoring as multiplication in reverse, finds the greatest
common factor of the terms of a polynomial (numbers and variables), factors it out, and
checks by distributing. Includes factoring out a negative and a binomial GCF.

**Topics.**
- Factoring = undoing multiplication.
- GCF of numbers and of variable parts (lowest exponent).
- Factoring out; checking by distributing.
- Factoring out −1 or a negative GCF.
- A binomial as a common factor; grouping.

#### S1 · Running it backwards
*Screen:* `3x(x + 4) → 3x² + 12x` with an arrow "multiply"; then the reverse arrow "factor".
> Multiplying three x by x plus four gives three x squared plus twelve x. Factoring runs that backwards: starting from three x squared plus twelve x, we find the factors that multiply to it.
> Why would anyone want to undo a multiplication? What could the factored form show that the expanded form hides?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* both forms side by side; countdown ring.
> Factored form shows the building blocks. Later, it will tell us where a function equals zero. For now, it's about seeing structure.

#### S2 · The greatest common factor
*Screen:* `12x³y² + 18x²y`: numbers 12 and 18 → GCF 6; x³ and x² → x²; y² and y → y; GCF `6x²y`.
> In twelve x cubed y squared plus eighteen x squared y, find what every term shares. The greatest number dividing twelve and eighteen is six. Both terms have at least x squared, and at least one y. The greatest common factor is six x squared y.
*Screen:* `6x²y(2xy + 3)`.
> Divide each term by it: twelve x cubed y squared over six x squared y is two x y, and eighteen x squared y over six x squared y is three. So the expression is six x squared y times the quantity two x y plus three.
*Screen:* check: `6x²y · 2xy + 6x²y · 3` = original ✓.
> Check by distributing: you get back the original.
> For the variables, why do we take the lowest exponent and not the highest?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `x³` and `x²`; countdown ring.
> Because the factor must divide every term. X squared fits into x cubed, but x cubed doesn't fit into x squared.

#### S3 · Negatives and leftover ones
*Screen:* `−4a² + 8a` → `−4a(a − 2)`.
> When the leading coefficient is negative, it's often neater to factor out a negative. Negative four a squared plus eight a is negative four a times the quantity a minus two. Notice the sign inside changes.
*Screen:* `5x² + 5x = 5x(x + 1)`; the `1` glows.
> And when a term is exactly the GCF, it leaves a one behind. Five x squared plus five x is five x times the quantity x plus one, not x plus nothing.

#### S4 · A binomial in common
*Screen:* `x(y + 2) + 7(y + 2)` → `(y + 2)(x + 7)`.
> The common factor can be a whole binomial. In x times the quantity y plus two, plus seven times the quantity y plus two, both terms share y plus two. Factor it out: y plus two, times x plus seven.
*Screen:* grouping: `xy + 2x + 7y + 14` → `x(y + 2) + 7(y + 2)` → `(y + 2)(x + 7)`.
> With four terms, you can sometimes create that by grouping: factor the first pair and the second pair, and a common binomial appears.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) `15m⁴ − 10m²` (b) `−6x³ − 9x² + 3x` (c) `a³ + 3a² + 4a + 12`.
> Your turn. Factor fifteen m to the fourth minus ten m squared, negative six x cubed minus nine x squared plus three x, and a cubed plus three a squared plus four a plus twelve. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `5m²(3m² − 2)` · `−3x(2x² + 3x − 1)` · `a²(a + 3) + 4(a + 3) = (a + 3)(a² + 4)`.
> Five m squared times three m squared minus two. Negative three x times two x squared plus three x minus one. And by grouping, a plus three times a squared plus four.
> Remember: factor out the greatest common factor first, always, and check by multiplying back.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 10.2 · Factoring x² + bx + c
- **Video:** `u10-l02-factoring-x2-bx-c`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:15 (speech 2:18 · pauses 42 s · 345 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana factors trinomials with leading coefficient 1 by finding two numbers whose
product is c and whose sum is b, using sign reasoning to narrow the search, and recognizes
prime trinomials.

**Topics.**
- Why: `(x + p)(x + q) = x² + (p + q)x + pq`.
- Product c, sum b.
- Sign patterns.
- Prime (unfactorable) trinomials.

#### S1 · Where the numbers come from
*Screen:* `(x + 2)(x + 5)` area box → `x² + 5x + 2x + 10` → `x² + 7x + 10`.
> Multiply x plus two times x plus five: x squared plus seven x plus ten. Look where the seven and the ten came from.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* 2 and 5 highlighted; 7 and 10 highlighted; countdown ring.
*Screen:* `(x + p)(x + q) = x² + (p + q)x + pq`.
> Seven is two plus five. Ten is two times five. In general, x plus p times x plus q is x squared plus the quantity p plus q times x, plus p q. To factor, we run it backwards: find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b.

#### S2 · The search
*Screen:* `x² + 9x + 20`: factor pairs of 20: 1·20 (21), 2·10 (12), 4·5 (9 ✓).
> To factor x squared plus nine x plus twenty, list the pairs that multiply to twenty: one and twenty, two and ten, four and five. Which pair adds to nine? Four and five.
*Screen:* `(x + 4)(x + 5)`.
> So it's x plus four times x plus five.

#### S3 · Signs
*Screen:* sign table: c > 0, b > 0 → both +; c > 0, b < 0 → both −; c < 0 → opposite signs, the larger has b's sign.
> Signs narrow the search. If c is positive, both numbers have the same sign, the sign of b. If c is negative, the numbers have opposite signs.
*Screen:* `x² − 8x + 12` → −2, −6 → `(x − 2)(x − 6)`.
> X squared minus eight x plus twelve: c is positive and b is negative, so both are negative. Negative two and negative six multiply to twelve and add to negative eight.
*Screen:* `x² + 3x − 28` → −4, 7 → `(x − 4)(x + 7)`.
> X squared plus three x minus twenty-eight: c is negative, so opposite signs, and the larger one is positive. Seven and negative four.
> Why must the numbers have opposite signs when c is negative?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `(+)(−) = −`; countdown ring.
> Because only a positive times a negative gives a negative product.

#### S4 · When nothing works
*Screen:* `x² + 4x + 6`: pairs 1·6 (7), 2·3 (5) → none add to 4 → "prime".
> Sometimes no pair works. For x squared plus four x plus six, the pairs are one and six, two and three. Neither adds to four. This trinomial can't be factored with integers. It's called prime.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) `x² − x − 30` (b) `x² − 13x + 36` (c) `2x² + 14x + 24` (hint: GCF first).
> Your turn. Factor x squared minus x minus thirty, x squared minus thirteen x plus thirty-six, and two x squared plus fourteen x plus twenty-four. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `(x − 6)(x + 5)` · `(x − 4)(x − 9)` · `2(x² + 7x + 12) = 2(x + 3)(x + 4)`.
> X minus six times x plus five. X minus four times x minus nine. And for the third, take out the two first: two times x plus three times x plus four.
> Remember: find two numbers with product c and sum b, let the signs guide you, and take out a common factor first.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 10.3 · Factoring ax² + bx + c
- **Video:** `u10-l03-factoring-ax2-bx-c`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:36 (speech 2:35 · pauses 47 s · 388 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana factors trinomials with leading coefficient other than 1 using the *ac*
method (split the middle term, then group), explains why it works, and checks by multiplying.

**Topics.**
- Why guessing gets harder.
- The ac method: product ac, sum b.
- Splitting the middle term and grouping.
- Why it works: it's the area box filled in.

#### S1 · Harder guesses
*Screen:* `2x² + 7x + 3` = `(2x + ?)(x + ?)`; several wrong guesses flash.
> When the leading coefficient isn't one, guessing gets harder. Two x squared plus seven x plus three must be two x plus something, times x plus something, but which numbers go where? The ac method makes it systematic.

#### S2 · The ac method
*Screen:* `a = 2, b = 7, c = 3`; `ac = 6`; pairs of 6 with sum 7: 1 and 6 ✓.
> Multiply a by c: two times three is six. Find two numbers that multiply to six and add to seven, the b. One and six.
*Screen:* `2x² + 1x + 6x + 3` → `x(2x + 1) + 3(2x + 1)` → `(2x + 1)(x + 3)`.
> Use them to split the middle term: two x squared plus x plus six x plus three. Now group: x times two x plus one, plus three times two x plus one. The common binomial comes out: two x plus one, times x plus three.
*Screen:* check by multiplying ✓.
> Check: two x squared plus six x plus x plus three. It works.
> Why do the two numbers need to multiply to a times c, and not just to c?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the area box for `(2x + 1)(x + 3)`: `2x²`, `6x`, `x`, `3`; the diagonals 2x²·3 and 6x·x highlighted; countdown ring.
> Look at the area box. The two diagonal boxes always have equal products: two x squared times three, and six x times x, are both six x squared. So the middle pieces multiply to a times c. That's the whole trick.

#### S3 · With negatives
*Screen:* `6x² − x − 2`: `ac = −12`; pairs with sum −1: 3 and −4 → `6x² + 3x − 4x − 2` → `3x(2x + 1) − 2(2x + 1)` → `(2x + 1)(3x − 2)`.
> Now six x squared minus x minus two. A times c is negative twelve. Which pair multiplies to negative twelve and adds to negative one?
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
> Three and negative four. Split: six x squared plus three x minus four x minus two. Group: three x times two x plus one, minus two times two x plus one. Factored: two x plus one, times three x minus two.
> When you group, watch the sign in front of the second pair. Why did we factor out negative two, and not positive two?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `−4x − 2` → `−2(2x + 1)`; countdown ring.
> So that the binomial left inside, two x plus one, matches the first group. If the binomials don't match, check your signs.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) `3x² + 10x + 8` (b) `4x² − 4x − 15`.
> Your turn. Factor three x squared plus ten x plus eight, and four x squared minus four x minus fifteen. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* (a) ac = 24 → 4, 6 → `(3x + 4)(x + 2)` · (b) ac = −60 → 6, −10 → `(2x + 3)(2x − 5)`.
> For the first, a c is twenty-four, and four and six add to ten: three x plus four times x plus two. For the second, a c is negative sixty, and six and negative ten add to negative four: two x plus three times two x minus five.
> Remember: multiply a times c, find the pair that adds to b, split the middle term, and group.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 10.4 · Difference of squares and perfect-square trinomials
- **Video:** `u10-l04-special-factoring`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:17 (speech 2:21 · pauses 42 s · 353 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana recognizes and factors differences of squares and perfect-square
trinomials by their structure, knows the sum of squares does not factor over the integers,
and factors completely when patterns repeat.

**Topics.**
- `a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b)`.
- `a² + 2ab + b² = (a + b)²` and `a² − 2ab + b² = (a − b)²`.
- Recognizing perfect squares (`9x²`, `16y⁴`).
- Sum of squares; factoring completely.

#### S1 · Reading special products backwards
*Screen:* `(x + 7)(x − 7) = x² − 49` reversed.
> In unit nine, a sum times a difference gave a difference of squares. Read it backwards, and any difference of two squares factors at once.
*Screen:* `a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b)`; `x² − 49 = (x + 7)(x − 7)`; `9y² − 25 = (3y + 5)(3y − 5)`.
> X squared minus forty-nine is x plus seven times x minus seven. Nine y squared minus twenty-five is three y plus five, times three y minus five, because nine y squared is three y squared.

#### S2 · The sum of squares
*Screen:* `x² + 49 = (x + 7)(x − 7)?` ✗; `(x + 7)² = x² + 14x + 49` ✗.
> What about x squared plus forty-nine? Try to find factors that work.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
> It doesn't factor with real numbers. Plus seven and minus seven give minus forty-nine. Both plus give a middle term. A sum of squares is prime.

#### S3 · Perfect-square trinomials
*Screen:* `x² + 10x + 25`: first `x²` ✓ square, last `25 = 5²` ✓, middle `2 · x · 5 = 10x` ✓ → `(x + 5)²`.
> A trinomial whose first and last terms are squares might be a perfect square. In x squared plus ten x plus twenty-five, x squared and twenty-five are squares of x and five. Is the middle term twice their product? Two times x times five is ten x. Yes. So it's x plus five, squared.
*Screen:* `4m² − 12m + 9` → `(2m − 3)²`, check `2 · 2m · 3 = 12m`.
> Four m squared minus twelve m plus nine: the squares of two m and three, and twice their product is twelve m, with a minus. So it's two m minus three, squared.
> Is x squared plus six x plus four a perfect square? Check the middle term.
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `x² + 6x + 4`; countdown ring.
> No. The squares are of x and two, and twice their product is four x, not six x.

#### S4 · Factoring completely
*Screen:* `2x³ − 18x` → `2x(x² − 9)` → `2x(x + 3)(x − 3)`; `x⁴ − 16` → `(x² + 4)(x² − 4)` → `(x² + 4)(x + 2)(x − 2)`.
> Always take out the GCF first, then look for patterns, and keep going until nothing factors. Two x cubed minus eighteen x is two x times x squared minus nine, which is two x times x plus three times x minus three. And x to the fourth minus sixteen factors twice.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) `49 − 4k²` (b) `9x² + 30x + 25` (c) `3a² − 75`.
> Your turn. Factor forty-nine minus four k squared, nine x squared plus thirty x plus twenty-five, and three a squared minus seventy-five, completely. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `(7 + 2k)(7 − 2k)` · `(3x + 5)²` · `3(a + 5)(a − 5)`.
> Seven plus two k times seven minus two k. Three x plus five, squared. And three times a plus five times a minus five.
> Remember: a difference of squares splits into a sum and a difference, a perfect-square trinomial is a binomial squared, and a sum of squares doesn't factor.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 10.5 · Factoring to find zeros
- **Video:** `u10-l05-zeros-by-factoring`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:08 (speech 2:12 · pauses 42 s · 331 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana uses the zero product property to find the zeros of a factored
polynomial, connects zeros to x-intercepts, and explains why the equation must equal zero
first.

**Topics.**
- Zero product property.
- Zeros of a function = x-intercepts.
- Factor, set each factor to zero.
- Why it only works with zero.

#### S1 · A product that is zero
*Screen:* `a · b = 0`.
> If two numbers multiply to zero, what can you say about them?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
> At least one of them must be zero. There's no other way to get a product of zero. This is the zero product property.
*Screen:* `(x − 3)(x + 5) = 0` → `x − 3 = 0` or `x + 5 = 0` → `x = 3` or `x = −5`.
> So if x minus three times x plus five equals zero, then x minus three is zero, or x plus five is zero. X is three or negative five.

#### S2 · Zeros and intercepts
*Screen:* graph of `f(x) = (x − 3)(x + 5)`; it crosses the x-axis at −5 and 3.
> For the function f of x equals x minus three times x plus five, the inputs that make it zero are three and negative five. These are the zeros of the function.
> On the graph, they're where the curve crosses the x axis, the x intercepts. Factored form shows them instantly.
*Screen:* `f(x) = x² + 2x − 15` → factored `(x − 3)(x + 5)`.
> The same function written as x squared plus two x minus fifteen hides them. Factoring reveals them.

#### S3 · Solving by factoring
*Screen:* `x² − 7x = −10` → `x² − 7x + 10 = 0` → `(x − 2)(x − 5) = 0` → `x = 2, 5`.
> To solve x squared minus seven x equals negative ten, first move everything to one side so it equals zero: x squared minus seven x plus ten equals zero. Factor: x minus two times x minus five. So x is two or five.
*Screen:* wrong path: `x(x − 7) = −10` → `x = −10` or `x − 7 = −10` ✗.
> Why can't you factor x times the quantity x minus seven equals negative ten, and set each factor equal to negative ten?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the wrong path in red; countdown ring.
> Because two numbers can multiply to negative ten in endless ways: two and negative five, one half and negative twenty, and so on. Only zero forces one factor to be zero.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) `3x² − 12x = 0` (b) `2x² + 5x = 3`.
> Your turn. Find the zeros of three x squared minus twelve x, and solve two x squared plus five x equals three. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* (a) `3x(x − 4) = 0` → `x = 0, 4` · (b) `2x² + 5x − 3 = 0` → `(2x − 1)(x + 3) = 0` → `x = 1/2, −3`.
> The first factors as three x times x minus four: zeros at zero and four. For the second, move the three over and factor: two x minus one times x plus three, so x is one half or negative three.
> Remember: set the equation equal to zero, factor, and set each factor to zero. The zeros are the x intercepts.
> That's the end of factoring. Next, quadratic functions.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.
