## 5.1 · Solving systems by graphing
- **Video:** `u05-l01-systems-by-graphing`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:03 (speech 2:03 · pauses 47 s · 307 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana understands that the solution of a system is a point that satisfies every
equation, finds it as the intersection of the graphs, and checks it in both equations.

**Topics.**
- A system: two conditions at once.
- The solution is an ordered pair that works in both.
- Intersection of the lines.
- Checking; limits of reading a graph.

#### S1 · Two clues
*Screen:* "Two numbers add to 10. Their difference is 4."
> Two numbers add up to ten, and their difference is four. What are they?
[THINK 5s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `x + y = 10`, `x − y = 4` with a brace.
> Seven and three. Each clue alone has infinitely many answers, but together they pin down one. Two equations that must be true at the same time form a system.
> The solution of a system is a pair of values, here x equals seven and y equals three, that makes every equation true.

#### S2 · Where the lines meet
*Screen:* graph `x + y = 10` (blue) and `x − y = 4` (green); they cross at (7, 3).
> Remember that a graph is the set of all solutions of an equation. The blue line holds every pair that adds to ten. The green line holds every pair with a difference of four.
> So which point is on both lists? Think before I show it.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* both lines; countdown ring.
*Screen:* the crossing (7, 3) glows.
> The only point on both lines is where they cross: seven, three. Solving a system by graphing means finding the intersection.

#### S3 · Check it
*Screen:* `7 + 3 = 10` ✓, `7 − 3 = 4` ✓.
> Always check in both equations. Seven plus three is ten. Seven minus three is four. Both true.
*Screen:* `y = 2x − 1` and `y = −x + 4`, crossing near (1.67, 2.33).
> Graphs can be imprecise. Here the lines cross somewhere near one point seven, two point three. When the crossing isn't on a grid point, graphing gives an estimate, and the next two methods give the exact answer.
> Why might someone still want to graph first, even if algebra is more exact?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
> A graph shows you the whole picture: whether there's a solution, roughly where it is, and if your algebra answer is reasonable.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* `y = −2x + 5`, `y = x − 4`.
> Your turn. Graph y equals negative two x plus five and y equals x minus four, find where they meet, and check the point. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* intersection (3, −1); checks: `−6 + 5 = −1` ✓, `3 − 4 = −1` ✓.
> They meet at three, negative one. Negative two times three plus five is negative one, and three minus four is negative one. It checks.
> Remember: the solution of a system is the point on every graph, where the lines intersect, and you always check it in every equation.

## 5.2 · Solving systems by substitution
- **Video:** `u05-l02-substitution`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:13 (speech 2:18 · pauses 40 s · 345 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana solves a system by isolating one variable and substituting its expression
into the other equation, choosing the easiest variable to isolate, and finishes by finding
both coordinates.

**Topics.**
- Idea: replace a variable with something equal to it.
- Isolate, substitute, solve, back-substitute.
- Choosing which variable to isolate.
- Parentheses when substituting.

#### S1 · Swapping in what's equal
*Screen:* "A burger costs $3 more than a drink. A burger and a drink cost $11."
> A burger costs three dollars more than a drink. Together they cost eleven dollars.
*Screen:* `b = d + 3`, `b + d = 11`.
> The first equation tells us b is the same as d plus three. So in the second equation, we can replace b with d plus three.
*Screen:* `(d + 3) + d = 11` → `2d + 3 = 11` → `d = 4` → `b = 7`.
> D plus three plus d equals eleven. Two d plus three equals eleven, so d is four. The drink costs four dollars, and the burger seven.
> Why is it allowed to replace b with d plus three?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `b = d + 3` with an arrow; countdown ring.
> Because they're equal. Swapping a quantity for something equal to it can't change whether an equation is true. One equation now has only one variable.

#### S2 · The four steps
*Screen:* `3x + y = 7`, `2x − 5y = 16`; step labels: 1 Isolate · 2 Substitute · 3 Solve · 4 Back-substitute.
> Here's the method on a harder system. Three x plus y equals seven, and two x minus five y equals sixteen.
> Which variable would you isolate first? Look at the coefficients.
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the `y` with coefficient 1 glows faintly; countdown ring.
*Screen:* `y = 7 − 3x`.
> Y in the first equation has a coefficient of one, so it's the easiest: y equals seven minus three x.
*Screen:* `2x − 5(7 − 3x) = 16` with the parentheses in blue.
> Substitute it into the other equation, with parentheses: two x minus five times the quantity seven minus three x equals sixteen.
*Screen:* `2x − 35 + 15x = 16` → `17x = 51` → `x = 3`.
> Distribute carefully: two x minus thirty-five plus fifteen x. Seventeen x minus thirty-five equals sixteen. Seventeen x equals fifty-one. X equals three.
*Screen:* `y = 7 − 9 = −2` → `(3, −2)`; checks.
> Back-substitute: y equals seven minus nine, negative two. The solution is three, negative two. Check: nine minus two is seven, and six plus ten is sixteen.

#### S3 · The parentheses trap
*Screen:* wrong: `2x − 5 · 7 − 3x = 16` in red.
> The most common mistake is forgetting the parentheses. Without them, the negative five multiplies only the seven, and the answer comes out wrong. The whole expression replaces y, so the whole expression gets multiplied.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* `x − 2y = 1`, `4x + 3y = 26`.
> Your turn. Solve x minus two y equals one and four x plus three y equals twenty-six by substitution. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `x = 1 + 2y` → `4(1 + 2y) + 3y = 26` → `11y = 22` → `y = 2`, `x = 5`.
> Isolate x: x equals one plus two y. Substitute: four times the quantity one plus two y, plus three y equals twenty-six. Eleven y plus four equals twenty-six, so y is two, and x is five. The solution is five, two.
> Remember: isolate the easiest variable, substitute it in parentheses, solve, then go back for the other variable.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 5.3 · Solving systems by elimination
- **Video:** `u05-l03-elimination`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:23 (speech 2:26 · pauses 42 s · 366 words · 5 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana solves systems by adding or subtracting equations to eliminate a
variable, multiplying one or both equations first when needed, and explains why adding two
true equations gives a true equation with the same solution.

**Topics.**
- Opposite coefficients: add the equations.
- Why it works: adding equal amounts to both sides.
- Multiplying an equation first to create opposites.
- Multiplying both equations (least common multiple).

#### S1 · Adding equations
*Screen:* `x + y = 12`, `x − y = 2`.
> Look at x plus y equals twelve and x minus y equals two. The y terms are opposites: plus y and minus y.
*Screen:* adding vertically: `2x = 14` → `x = 7` → `y = 5`.
> Add the two equations, left side to left side and right side to right side. The y terms cancel. Two x equals fourteen, so x is seven, and then y is five.
> But wait. Why are we allowed to add two equations together?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* two balance scales, both level; countdown ring.

#### S2 · Why it works
*Screen:* scale 1: `x + y` | `12`. Scale 2: `x − y` | `2`. Put scale 2's left contents on scale 1's left, and its right on scale 1's right.
> The second equation says x minus y and two are equal amounts. Adding equal amounts to both sides of the first equation keeps it balanced. That's all adding equations is: the addition property of equality, with a clever choice of what to add.
> And any pair that solves both original equations still solves the new one, so the solution is the same.

#### S3 · Multiply first
*Screen:* `3x + 2y = 16`, `x − 4y = −18`.
> Here nothing cancels yet. Three x plus two y equals sixteen, and x minus four y equals negative eighteen.
> What could you multiply one equation by so that the y terms become opposites?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* `2y` and `−4y` glow; countdown ring.
*Screen:* first equation × 2: `6x + 4y = 32`; add: `7x = 14` → `x = 2` → `y = 5`.
> Multiply the first equation by two: six x plus four y equals thirty-two. Now plus four y and minus four y are opposites. Add: seven x equals fourteen. X is two. Back in the second equation, two minus four y equals negative eighteen, so y is five.

#### S4 · Multiply both
*Screen:* `4x + 3y = 1`, `3x − 2y = 5` → ×2 and ×3 → `8x + 6y = 2`, `9x − 6y = 15` → `17x = 17`.
> Sometimes both need multiplying. In four x plus three y equals one and three x minus two y equals five, multiply the first by two and the second by three. The y terms become plus six y and minus six y.
*Screen:* `x = 1`, `y = −1`.
> Add: seventeen x equals seventeen, so x is one. Then four plus three y equals one, so y is negative one.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* `5x + 2y = 4`, `3x + 2y = 0`.
> Your turn. Solve five x plus two y equals four and three x plus two y equals zero by elimination. Hint: here the y terms are equal, not opposite. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* subtract: `2x = 4` → `x = 2` → `6 + 2y = 0` → `y = −3`.
> Subtract the second equation from the first: two x equals four, so x is two. Then six plus two y equals zero, so y is negative three. The solution is two, negative three.
> Remember: make one variable's coefficients opposites, add the equations to eliminate it, and back-substitute. You're always adding equal amounts to both sides.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 5.4 · How many solutions?
- **Video:** `u05-l04-number-of-solutions`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 2:49 (speech 1:53 · pauses 42 s · 282 words · 5 scenes) · short

**Objective.** Ana determines whether a linear system has one, no, or infinitely many
solutions, from the graphs (crossing, parallel, same line), from slopes and intercepts, and
from what remains when solving algebraically.

**Topics.**
- Three ways two lines can sit.
- Slopes and intercepts predict the case.
- Algebra: false statement vs. identity.
- Meaning in context.

#### S1 · Three pictures
*Screen:* three graphs: crossing lines · parallel lines · one line drawn twice (thick).
> Two lines on a plane can do only three things. They cross once. They never meet. Or they're the same line.
> How many solutions does each picture show?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the three graphs; countdown ring.
> One solution, no solution, and infinitely many solutions, because every point of the line is on both.

#### S2 · Predicting with slopes
*Screen:* table: different slopes → one · same slope, different intercepts → none · same slope, same intercept → infinitely many.
> Put both equations in slope-intercept form. Different slopes: the lines must cross, one solution. Same slope but different intercepts: parallel, no solution. Same slope and same intercept: the same line, infinitely many.
*Screen:* `y = 3x − 2` and `6x − 2y = 4` → second becomes `y = 3x − 2`.
> For example, y equals three x minus two and six x minus two y equals four. Solve the second for y: it's also y equals three x minus two. Same line. Infinitely many solutions.

#### S3 · What algebra says
*Screen:* `x + 2y = 5`, `2x + 4y = 3`; elimination: double the first → `2x + 4y = 10`; subtract → `0 = 7`.
> Algebra tells you too. Double the first equation and subtract the second: zero equals seven. Everything vanished and a false statement remains.
> You've seen this before with one-variable equations. What does a false statement mean here?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* `0 = 7` in red; countdown ring.
> No pair x, y can make both equations true. The lines are parallel: no solution. If you'd gotten zero equals zero, it would be the same line, with infinitely many solutions.

#### S4 · In context
*Screen:* "Two gyms: both charge $10 per visit; one has a $20 fee, the other a $35 fee."
> In real life, no solution means something specific. Two gyms that charge the same per visit but different fees will never cost the same. Their cost lines are parallel.

#### S5 · Your turn
*Screen:* (a) `y = −x + 4`, `2x + 2y = 8` · (b) `y = 2x + 1`, `y = −2x + 1` · (c) `3x − y = 2`, `y = 3x + 5`.
> Your turn. Decide how many solutions each system has. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* (a) same line → infinitely many · (b) different slopes → one, (0, 1) · (c) same slope, different intercepts → none.
> The first is the same line twice: infinitely many. The second has different slopes: one solution, zero, one. The third has slope three twice but intercepts negative two and five: no solution.
> Remember: crossing lines, one solution; parallel lines, none; the same line, infinitely many. Slopes and intercepts tell you which before you solve.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 5.5 · Linear inequalities in two variables
- **Video:** `u05-l05-inequalities-two-variables`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 3:01 (speech 2:06 · pauses 42 s · 316 words · 4 scenes)

**Objective.** Ana graphs a linear inequality in two variables as a half-plane, chooses a
solid or dashed boundary, picks the side with a test point, and writes an inequality from a
situation.

**Topics.**
- Solutions are points; they fill a region.
- Boundary line: dashed for `<`, `>`; solid for `≤`, `≥`.
- Test point to choose the side.
- Context: budgets.

#### S1 · A snack budget
*Screen:* "Chips cost $2, drinks cost $3. You have at most $12." `2x + 3y ≤ 12`.
> Chips cost two dollars and drinks three. You can spend at most twelve dollars. If x is bags of chips and y is drinks, then two x plus three y is less than or equal to twelve.
*Screen:* points (0, 0), (3, 2), (6, 0) green; (4, 3) red.
> Zero and zero works. Three chips and two drinks costs twelve: it works. Four chips and three drinks costs seventeen: too much.
> Where on the plane do you think all the working points are?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the plotted points; countdown ring.

#### S2 · The boundary and the half-plane
*Screen:* the line `2x + 3y = 12` through (6, 0) and (0, 4); the region below it shades.
> The line two x plus three y equals twelve is where you spend exactly twelve dollars. It splits the plane into two halves. On one side you spend less, on the other you spend more.
> The solutions fill a half-plane: all the points on one side of the line, plus the line itself, because spending exactly twelve is allowed.
*Screen:* solid vs. dashed lines with their symbols.
> Draw the boundary solid for less than or equal to and greater than or equal to. Draw it dashed for strict inequalities, because the line itself isn't included.

#### S3 · Which side?
*Screen:* `y > −2x + 1`; dashed line; test point (0, 0): `0 > 1` ✗ → shade the other side.
> To choose the side, test a point that isn't on the line. Zero, zero is easiest. Is zero greater than negative two times zero plus one? Zero is not greater than one. So zero, zero is not a solution: shade the other side.
> What if the line passes through zero, zero? What would you do then?
[REFLECT 10s] *Screen:* the line `y = 3x` through the origin; countdown ring.
> Pick a different test point, like one, zero. Any point off the line works.

#### S4 · Your turn
*Screen:* `x − 2y ≥ 4`.
> Your turn. Graph x minus two y is greater than or equal to four. Decide solid or dashed, and test a point. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* solid line through (4, 0) and (0, −2); test (0, 0): `0 ≥ 4` ✗ → shade below/right.
> The boundary goes through four, zero and zero, negative two, and it's solid. Zero, zero gives zero greater than or equal to four, which is false, so shade the side away from the origin, below the line.
> Remember: draw the boundary, solid or dashed, then test a point to shade the half-plane of solutions.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.

## 5.6 · Systems of inequalities and constraints
- **Video:** `u05-l06-systems-of-inequalities`
- **Status:** script
- **Estimated length:** 2:52 (speech 1:57 · pauses 44 s · 293 words · 3 scenes) · short

**Objective.** Ana graphs a system of linear inequalities, identifies the overlap as the
solution region, writes constraints from a real problem, and judges whether particular
combinations are viable.

**Topics.**
- The solution region is the overlap.
- Writing constraints from a situation (including x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0).
- Viable and non-viable points.
- Corner points as the extreme options.

#### S1 · A bake sale
*Screen:* "Cookies take 1 cup of flour, cakes take 3 cups; 15 cups available. We can bake at most 9 items."
> For a bake sale, a batch of cookies needs one cup of flour, and a cake needs three. You have fifteen cups, and oven time for at most nine items.
> What conditions limit what you can bake? Try to name all of them, even the obvious ones.
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* `c + 3k ≤ 15` · `c + k ≤ 9` · `c ≥ 0` · `k ≥ 0`.
> Flour: c plus three k is at most fifteen. Oven: c plus k is at most nine. And the obvious ones: you can't bake a negative number of anything, so c and k are both at least zero. Conditions like these are called constraints.

#### S2 · The overlap
*Screen:* each inequality shades in its own color; the overlap darkens into a four-sided region with corners (0, 0), (9, 0), (6, 3), (0, 5).
> Graph each constraint. Each one shades a half-plane. The points that satisfy all of them are where every shading overlaps. That region is the solution of the system.
*Screen:* points (4, 2) inside ✓; (8, 3) outside ✗ (flour 17).
> Four batches of cookies and two cakes is inside the region: it uses ten cups and six oven slots. Eight cookies and three cakes is outside: it needs seventeen cups of flour.
> Which point in the region is the most extreme: the most items with the most cakes?
[REFLECT 12s] *Screen:* the corners glow; countdown ring.
*Screen:* corner (6, 3): `6 + 9 = 15` cups, 9 items.
> The corner six, three uses all the flour and all the oven space. Corners are where the best and worst options usually are, which is why they matter in planning.

#### S3 · Your turn
*Screen:* `y ≥ x − 2`, `y < −x + 4`; points to test: (1, 1), (4, 0), (0, 5).
> Your turn. Graph y greater than or equal to x minus two, and y less than negative x plus four. Then decide which of these points are solutions: one, one; four, zero; and zero, five. Pause the video and try it.
[TRY IT 20s] *Screen:* countdown ring.
*Screen:* overlap shaded (solid and dashed boundaries); (1, 1) ✓ · (4, 0) ✗ (0 ≥ 2 false) · (0, 5) ✗ (5 < 4 false).
> One, one is in the overlap. Four, zero fails the first inequality. Zero, five fails the second.
> Remember: a system of inequalities is solved by the overlap of all the half-planes. In real problems, write every constraint, including the obvious ones.
> That's the end of systems. Next, two-variable statistics.
> This video uses an AI-generated voice.
